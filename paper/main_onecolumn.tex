\begin{abstract}
Users of multitaper spectral estimation must choose a time--bandwidth product $NW$ and a number of tapers $K$. Neither parameter has a counterpart in the two methods most people learn first, which are averaging the periodograms of segments and smoothing one periodogram across frequency. Building on an identity of Thomson (1982), we prove that the three methods are one estimator, a bank of tapers with weights that is built from a single window by shifting it in time, shifting it in frequency, or taking orthogonal tapers. The multitaper estimate that uses all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, equals the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$. In the limit of a long window it also equals the average of periodograms of a sinc window slid along the record. The usual estimator with $K\approx2NW-1$ tapers is this estimate with its leaky part removed. We quantify three consequences over 300 realizations of a spectrum spanning 65~dB. Smoothing does not remove leakage. Where the spectrum is low, the untapered smoothed periodogram errs by 14~dB and the Hann-tapered one by 1.5~dB. A single taper has a third fewer degrees of freedom ($\nu=8.9$ against $14.0$) because its bank has unequal weights. The last Slepian taper leaks, so that with default settings the multitaper estimate errs by 6~dB where the spectrum is low. Adaptive weights repair this at the cost of half the degrees of freedom there. We give a rule for choosing the bandwidth, a recipe for each route, tested code, and a demonstration on scalp EEG containing seizures, where the routes agree to a median of 1.5~dB.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Three families of spectral estimator are in common use in electrophysiology. The first averages periodograms. The record is cut into segments, a periodogram is computed for each, and the periodograms are averaged. This is Bartlett's method \cite{bartlett1948}, or Welch's \cite{welch1967} when the segments are tapered and overlap, and textbooks such as Kay's \cite{kay1988} present it first. The second smooths a periodogram. One periodogram of the whole record is convolved across frequency with a box (Daniell \cite{daniell1946}) or a Gaussian. This is the Blackman--Tukey estimator \cite{blackman1958}, and many analysis pipelines apply it to spectrograms without naming it. The third is Thomson's multitaper method \cite{thomson1982}, which two tutorials for neuroscientists \cite{babadi2014,prerau2017} recommend because it controls leakage, reduces variance, and is optimal in a defined sense. Its two parameters, however, have no counterpart in the first two methods. A user who knows how to choose a segment length or a smoothing width has no obvious way to decide what time--bandwidth product to use, or whether to keep seven tapers or six. The tapers themselves, Slepian's discrete prolate spheroidal sequences \cite{slepian1978}, are also unfamiliar to most users.

We show that the three methods are the same estimator (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract}). Each is a quadratic form in the data, and a quadratic form can be written as a bank of tapers with weights. Averaging periodograms builds the bank by shifting one window along the record. Smoothing builds it by shifting one taper along the frequency axis. Thomson's method builds it from orthogonal tapers. Each bank has an expected-value kernel and an equivalent number of degrees of freedom, so the three methods can be compared on the same terms. One of the correspondences is exact. The multitaper estimate with all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, equals the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$. This is equation (8.3) of Thomson's paper \cite{thomson1982}, and neither tutorial mentions it. Averaging the periodograms of a sliding window is itself a form of smoothing \cite{welch1967,nuttall1982}, so sliding a sinc window across the record produces the same estimate, and the Slepian tapers are the principal components of that sliding window.

These results have three practical consequences, which we measured over 300 realizations of a spectrum spanning 65~dB. Leakage survives smoothing and averaging. Where the spectrum is low, a box-smoothed periodogram errs by 14~dB without a taper and by 1.5~dB with one. A single taper has a third fewer degrees of freedom than the multitaper estimate at the same bandwidth ($\nu=8.9$ against $14.0$), which raises the error at the spectral peaks from 1.8 to 2.3~dB. With default settings the multitaper estimate errs by 6~dB where the spectrum is low, and by 15~dB on records of 128 samples, while the tapered, smoothed periodogram errs by 1.5~dB. Adaptive weights remove this difference.

The paper makes five contributions. We prove that averaging periodograms, smoothing a periodogram and multitaper estimation are one estimator (Section~\ref{sec:one}). We derive the sliding-window form of Thomson's identity and show that the Slepian sequences are the principal components of a sinc window slid across the record (Section~\ref{sec:sliding}, Appendix~\ref{app:welch}). We show that the variance cost of a single taper is determined by the slope of its eigen-weight ladder, and would arise with any single taper. We compute exact kernels, leakage and degrees of freedom for ten estimators at matched bandwidth, together with their errors over an ensemble (Tables~\ref{tab:ensemble} and \ref{tab:dof}). We give a recipe for each route and a rule for choosing its bandwidth (Sections~\ref{sec:howwide} and \ref{sec:recipe}), and we test both on scalp EEG containing focal seizures (Section~\ref{sec:eeg}).

The method is the same throughout. We write each estimator as a quadratic form, diagonalize it, and compare estimators through their kernels and eigen-weight ladders. Sections~\ref{sec:periodogram} and \ref{sec:fixes} introduce the kernel picture and the three classical remedies. Section~\ref{sec:mt} presents multitaper estimation in the usual way. Section~\ref{sec:one} shows that the three methods are one and quantifies how the versions used in practice differ. Sections~\ref{sec:recipe} and \ref{sec:eeg} give the recipes and the EEG demonstration. Proofs are in the appendices.

\section{The periodogram and the kernel picture}
\label{sec:periodogram}

Every estimator in this paper returns the true spectrum blurred by a kernel, plus noise. For the periodogram the kernel is Fej\'er's and the noise is at its largest. Let $x_0,\dots,x_{N-1}$ be $N$ samples of a stationary signal. We take the sampling interval as the unit of time, so that frequency $f$ is in cycles per sample and runs from $-\tfrac12$ to $\tfrac12$. The periodogram is
\begin{equation}
\hat I(f) = \frac{1}{N}\Bigl|\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} x_t\, e^{-i2\pi f t}\Bigr|^2 ,
\label{eq:periodogram}
\end{equation}
the squared magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform. Zero-padding before the fast Fourier transform (FFT) evaluates it on as fine a grid as needed. It has two well-known defects, both visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}(a,\,e).

\paragraph{Noise.} At any one frequency, the periodogram of a Gaussian signal is distributed as the true power spectral density (PSD) multiplied by a chi-square variable with two degrees of freedom, divided by two. Its standard deviation therefore equals its mean. A longer record does not reduce the noise, because it adds frequency points that are each as noisy as before.

\paragraph{Leakage.} The expected value of the periodogram is the true PSD $S$ convolved with a kernel,
\begin{equation}
\E[\hat I(f)] = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} F(f-f')\,S(f')\dd f' ,
\qquad F(f)=\frac{1}{N}\biggl(\frac{\sin N\pi f}{\sin \pi f}\biggr)^{2},
\label{eq:fejer}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is Fej\'er's kernel, the squared Fourier transform of the rectangular window that truncated the record. $F$ has a main lobe about $2/N$ wide and side lobes that decay only as $1/f^2$. Neural spectra vary by tens of decibels across the band. The tails of $F$ then carry power from the strong part of the spectrum into the weak part, where the estimate is set by leakage instead of by the signal.

\paragraph{The kernel picture.} Equation~\eqref{eq:fejer} generalizes. Every estimator in this paper satisfies
\begin{equation}
\E[\hat S(f)] = \int H(f-f')\,S(f')\dd f' ,
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the \emph{expected-value kernel} of the estimator, and the estimate fluctuates around this expected value. Three quantities characterize an estimator. Its resolution is the width of the main lobe of $H$. Its leakage is the height of the skirts of $H$. Its variance is reported as an equivalent number of degrees of freedom $\nu$, meaning that the estimate behaves like $S\,\chi^2_\nu/\nu$. The relative standard error is then $\sqrt{2/\nu}$, or about $4.3\sqrt{2/\nu}$~dB, and an approximate 95\% confidence interval runs from $\nu\hat S/\chi^2_{\nu,0.975}$ to $\nu\hat S/\chi^2_{\nu,0.025}$. The periodogram has the narrowest main lobe possible, high leakage, and $\nu=2$. Each remedy below improves one of the three quantities at the expense of another. Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic} gives the formulas used to compute the kernel and $\nu$ of each estimator exactly, and all numbers quoted below come from them.

\section{Three classical remedies}
\label{sec:fixes}

\subsection{Averaging the periodograms of segments}
\label{sec:averaging}
Bartlett's remedy for the noise \cite{bartlett1948} is to cut the record into $M$ segments of length $L$, compute a periodogram of each, and average them. Segments that do not overlap give independent estimates with $\nu=2$ each, so the average has $\nu\approx2M$. Resolution is lost in exchange. The kernel of each segment is the Fej\'er kernel of length $L$, whose main lobe is $2/L=2M/N$ wide, and averaging does not narrow it. Welch \cite{welch1967} tapers each segment to control leakage and overlaps the segments by half their length to recover the data lost at the tapered ends. Hann segments at 50\% overlap are nearly uncorrelated \cite{welch1967,percival1993}, so $\nu$ is again close to twice the number of segments (13.4 for seven segments in Table~\ref{tab:dof}). Whatever the overlap, the kernel of the estimator is the spectral window of the segment taper, $|\mathcal{W}_L(f)|^2$. The segment length therefore sets the bandwidth, and using more and shorter segments raises $\nu$ and widens the kernel.

\subsection{Smoothing one periodogram}
\label{sec:smoothing}
The second remedy computes one periodogram of the whole record and averages it over a window of frequencies. The window may be a box of half-width $W$ \cite{daniell1946}, a Gaussian, or any non-negative kernel $G$. Because expectation commutes with averaging, the kernel of the smoothed estimate is the kernel of the unsmoothed one convolved with the smoothing window, $H=G*F$. The main lobe widens to about $2W$, which the user sets directly. The noise falls, because periodogram values $1/N$ apart are nearly independent and a band of width $2W$ pools about $2NW$ of them, giving $\nu\approx2\cdot2NW$. The leakage does not change, because convolution with a non-negative window redistributes the skirts and cannot cancel them. This is the Blackman--Tukey estimator \cite{blackman1958} computed in the frequency domain. Convolving a spectrogram with a Gaussian along the frequency axis is the same operation. Section~\ref{sec:sliding} shows that smoothing and averaging are the same remedy.

\subsection{Tapering}
\label{sec:tapering}
Averaging and smoothing do not reduce leakage, which is why both are usually applied to tapered periodograms. A taper is a window $w_t$ that falls smoothly to zero at both ends of the record, and the data are multiplied by it before the transform. The kernel becomes $|\mathcal{W}(f)|^2$, the squared transform of the taper. With a Hann window the first side lobe drops from $-13$ to $-31$~dB and the tails decay as $1/f^6$ instead of $1/f^2$, while the main lobe doubles in width (Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}f). Tapering does not reduce the variance, and a single tapered periodogram still has $\nu=2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}b). The taper sets how fast the skirts fall. The smoothing window, or the segment length, sets the width of the main lobe.

\subsection{Choosing the bandwidth}
\label{sec:howwide}
Each remedy requires a bandwidth, set by the segment length, by the half-width $W$, or by the time--bandwidth product of Section~\ref{sec:mt}. Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} shows how the choice affects a smoothed periodogram. A wider box pools more periodogram ordinates, so the noise falls as $4.3\sqrt{2/\nu}$~dB with $\nu$ roughly proportional to $NW$ (blue curve). A wider box also blurs the spectrum over a width $2W$. A peak narrower than $2W$ is lowered and broadened, and a valley narrower than $2W$ is partly filled. This bias is zero for a flat spectrum and grows with the curvature of $S$ on the scale of the box (orange curve). Noise and bias add in quadrature, and their total has a broad minimum where the two are comparable.

The position of the minimum depends on the narrowest feature that must be preserved. The peaks in Fig.~\ref{fig:motif} are $7/N$ wide, so a box with $2W\approx7/N$, that is $W=4/N$, is the widest that does not flatten them. With this box the estimate has $\nu=8.9$ and follows the true spectrum to within its own noise of about $\pm2$~dB. A narrower box gives a noisier estimate than necessary ($\nu=2$, error 6~dB). A box wider than the separation of the peaks merges them ($W=24/N$, $\nu=50$), and although the estimate looks clean, its error over the peak region is again 6~dB. The optimum also depends on the region that is scored. It is $W\approx8/N$ over the whole band, most of which has no features, and $W\approx4$ to $5/N$ over the peaks. For real data the optimum is therefore not known in advance. We suggest the rule that Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} illustrates. Identify the narrowest feature of interest, for example an alpha peak one to two hertz wide, and set $2W$ to about its width. Data-driven choices of bandwidth exist for smoothed periodograms \cite{wahba1980,hurvich1985} and for multitaper estimates \cite{haley2017}. They optimize a criterion over the whole band, so the same caveat applies.

\section{Multitaper estimation}
\label{sec:mt}

Thomson's method \cite{thomson1982} achieves low leakage and low variance together, without shortening the record, by using several orthogonal tapers. The user chooses a half-bandwidth $W$. The tapers are the sequences of length $N$ that are mutually orthogonal and whose transforms are most concentrated in $[-W,W]$. These are Slepian's sequences \cite{slepian1978}. There are $N$ of them, $v_0,\dots,v_{N-1}$, ordered by their concentrations $1>\lambda_0>\lambda_1>\dots>0$, where $\lambda_k$ is the fraction of the spectral energy of the $k$th taper that lies inside the band. For $NW=4$ the first six concentrations exceed $0.99$, the seventh is $\lambda_6=0.94$, and the rest fall rapidly toward zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}d). Each taper gives an eigenspectrum $\hat S_k(f)=|\sum_t v_{k,t}x_te^{-i2\pi ft}|^2$, and the estimate is the average of the first $K=2NW-1$ eigenspectra,
\begin{equation}
\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)=\frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\hat S_k(f).
\label{eq:mt}
\end{equation}

The method works because its kernel is close to a box. Each taper has low leakage, so each eigenspectrum has little bias. The tapers are orthogonal, so the eigenspectra are nearly uncorrelated and the average of $K$ of them has $\nu\approx2K$. The kernel of \eqref{eq:mt} is the average of the $K$ spectral windows $|U_k|^2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}b). The zeroth window is a single bump at the center of the band, the first has two bumps on either side of the center, and the second has three. Each window fills gaps left by the previous ones, and their sum is nearly flat over $[-W,W]$ and nearly zero outside it (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}c). Thomson's optimality result is that no other set of $K$ orthogonal tapers places more energy inside the band.

\section{The three methods as one estimator}
\label{sec:one}

\subsection{Every estimator is a bank of tapers}
\label{sec:banks}
Every estimator in this paper is a quadratic form in the data, and every quadratic form can be written as a bank of orthogonal tapers with weights. Let $x_f$ denote the data modulated to frequency $f$, with entries $x_te^{-i2\pi ft}$. Then $\hat S(f)=x_f^{*}Qx_f$ for a fixed real symmetric matrix $Q$ \cite{percival1993,riedel1995}. The eigendecomposition $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$, with orthonormal $v_k$, gives
\begin{equation}
\hat S(f)=\sum_k c_k\,\bigl|v_k^{\!\top}x_f\bigr|^2 ,
\label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
which has the form of a multitaper estimate with tapers $v_k$ and weights $c_k$. The three methods differ in how they construct the bank (Fig.~\ref{fig:banks}). Averaging periodograms places one window at $M$ offsets and adds the squared transforms with equal weight, so that $Q=\frac1M\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}$. The bank consists of \emph{time-shifted} copies of one window, which are orthogonal only if the segments do not overlap. Smoothing a tapered periodogram with a kernel $G$ gives $\int G(f')\,|(w\odot e_{f'})^{\!\top}x_f|^2\dd f'$. Here the bank consists of \emph{frequency-shifted} copies of one taper, weighted by the kernel. Thomson's method starts from an orthogonal bank.

The kernel and the degrees of freedom both follow from the bank. The kernel is $H=\sum_kc_k|V_k|^2$. At an interior frequency the degrees of freedom are
\begin{equation}
\nu=\frac{2\bigl(\sum_kc_k\bigr)^2}{\sum_kc_k^2},
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
which is Satterthwaite's approximation \cite{satterthwaite1946} applied to the bank and agrees with the exact value to within 3\% for the estimators we tested (Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic}). The value is $2K$ for $K$ equal weights and smaller when the weights are unequal. We call the ordered weights $c_0\ge c_1\ge\dots$ the \emph{eigen-weight ladder}. Thomson's ladder is flat by construction. The ladder of a smoothed single-taper periodogram slopes downward, because the modulated copies of one taper overlap strongly. This slope is the source of the variance cost of the single-taper route, and any single taper would produce it. The ladder of Welch's method with half-overlapping Hann segments is nearly flat, and the cost of that method appears in the shape of its kernel.

\subsection{Thomson's identity}
\label{sec:identity}
Suppose that all $N$ tapers are kept and each eigenspectrum is weighted by its concentration. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\frac{1}{2NW}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\lambda_k\,\hat S_k(f)\;=\;\frac{1}{2W}\int_{f-W}^{f+W}\hat I(f')\dd f'\;}
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The left side is the eigenvalue-weighted average of all $N$ eigenspectra, whose weights sum to $2NW$. The right side is the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$. This is Thomson's equation (8.3) \cite{thomson1982}. It holds for each record individually as well as in expectation, and we confirmed it to machine precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract}).

The proof is brief. The Slepian sequences are the eigenvectors of the $N\times N$ matrix $A$ with entries $\sin(2\pi W(t-t'))/(\pi(t-t'))$, and the $\lambda_k$ are its eigenvalues. The entries depend only on the lag $t-t'$, and as a function of lag they form a sinc, which is the inverse transform of the box. The matrix $A$ therefore represents the box kernel, and $A=\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ is its decomposition into orthogonal components. Evaluating $x_f^{*}Ax_f$ through the eigenvectors gives the left side of \eqref{eq:identity}, and evaluating it through the lags gives the right side (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}). In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:banks}, the Slepian tapers are the eigen-tapers of the box-smoothed periodogram. Their concentrations form its ladder, which is flat near one for $k<2NW$ and then falls to zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:banks}c).

\subsection{Averaging as smoothing}
\label{sec:sliding}
The matrix $A$ also arises from averaging. Consider a window $w$ that slides across the record one sample at a time and is allowed to extend beyond both ends of the zero-padded record. The squared transforms of the windowed data are summed over all positions. Appendix~\ref{app:welch} shows that the matrix $Q$ of this estimator has entries $r_w(t-t')$, where $r_w$ is the autocorrelation of the window. Averaging the periodograms of a sliding window is therefore exactly the same as smoothing the untapered periodogram with $|\mathcal{W}|^2$. For a sinc window $w_t=2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2Wt)$ the autocorrelation is $2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2W\tau)$, which is the lag sequence of $A$, so that
\begin{equation}
\sum_s\bigl|u_s^{\!\top}x_f\bigr|^2=x_f^{*}Ax_f=\sum_k\lambda_k\hat S_k(f),\qquad u_s=\text{the sinc window at offset }s,
\label{eq:sliding}
\end{equation}
The equality is exact for an infinitely long window. For a window of length $L$ the relative error in $Q$ is 3.5\% at $L=2N$, 1.5\% at $4N$ and 0.17\% at $32N$, and it decreases as $1/L$ once $L$ exceeds $2N$ (Appendix~\ref{app:welch}). The matrix $A=\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}$ is the scatter matrix of the shifted windows. Its eigenvectors, the Slepian sequences, are therefore the principal components of the sliding sinc window, and Thomson's estimator retains the leading components.

\subsection{The estimators used in practice}
\label{sec:everyday}
The standard estimate \eqref{eq:mt} differs from the left side of \eqref{eq:identity} in two ways. It uses equal weights, which changes little because $\lambda_k\approx1$ for $k<K$. It also stops at $K=2NW-1$ tapers, and this is the difference that matters. Rearranging \eqref{eq:identity} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{K}{2NW}\,\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)\;\approx\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{2W}\int_{f-W}^{f+W}\hat I(f')\dd f'}_{\text{box-smoothed periodogram}}\;-\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{2NW}\sum_{k\ge K}\lambda_k\hat S_k(f)}_{\text{leaky remainder}} .
\label{eq:corollary}
\end{equation}
The factor $K/2NW$, which is $7/8$ for $NW=4$, arises because the standard estimate averages over $K$ tapers while the weights in \eqref{eq:identity} sum to $2NW$. The eigenspectra that are dropped belong to tapers whose energy lies almost entirely outside $[-W,W]$, so each contributes at frequency $f$ only power that has leaked in from other frequencies. The multitaper estimate is thus the box-smoothed periodogram, or equivalently the sliding-sinc average, with this leakage subtracted. Thomson noted that the two agree ``only for white spectra'' \cite{thomson1982}.

We asked whether either of the other routes could achieve the same subtraction with a different window. Hansson-Sandsten posed this question for Welch's method as a least-squares problem, in which the matrix of the Thomson estimator is approximated by the matrix of a Welch estimator \cite{hansson2012}. We ran searches of this kind in both families, over segment windows of four lengths and three steps and over taper--kernel pairs by alternating least squares (\texttt{demos/fit\_single\_window.py}). Both searches returned approximately the sliding sinc window and the rectangular taper with the box. Their relative distance from the $K$-taper matrix was $0.27$ to $0.29$, compared with $0.86$ for the Hann taper with the box. Within these families, the single-window estimator closest to Thomson's is therefore the leaky one. The other routes can avoid leakage only by tapering before they smooth or average.

Fig.~\ref{fig:everyday} shows the estimators used in practice on the record of Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}. Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} gives their errors over 300 realizations in three regions of the spectrum, Table~\ref{tab:dof} gives their exact kernel properties, and Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg8s} shows them on EEG. The main findings are as follows.

\paragraph{Leakage survives smoothing and averaging.} The box-smoothed untapered periodogram has the intended resolution and the most degrees of freedom. Where the spectrum is low it errs by 13.7~dB (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}), because the leakage in the Fej\'er tails is averaged and remains in the estimate, as \eqref{eq:corollary} predicts. The same box applied to a Hann-tapered periodogram errs by 1.5~dB in that region. The taper controls the skirts of the kernel, and the box or segment length controls the width of its main lobe. Neither can substitute for the other. Prewhitening is the other classical remedy for a large dynamic range \cite{blackman1958,percival1993}, and it can be combined with any of the three routes.

\paragraph{A single taper costs degrees of freedom.} At the spectral peaks leakage is negligible and the errors follow $\nu$. The multitaper estimate, with $\nu=14.0$, errs by 1.8~dB. The Hann-tapered, box-smoothed periodogram has $\nu=8.9$, a third fewer, because its ladder slopes, and it errs by 2.3~dB. The corresponding relative standard errors $\sqrt{2/\nu}$ are 38\% and 47\%. The cost can be paid in resolution instead. If the box is widened until the Hann route reaches $\nu=14$, its kernel is $13/N$ wide, compared with $7.4/N$ for Thomson's. The deficit decreases as $N$ grows, because the broadening caused by a single taper scales as $1/N$ and $W$ does not \cite{riedel1994}.

\paragraph{The last Slepian taper leaks.} For $NW=4$ the seventh taper has $\lambda_6=0.94$, so 6\% of its energy lies outside the band. This taper sets the skirt of the multitaper kernel at $-22$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:everyday}f), well above the $-36$~dB of the Hann taper. With equal weights and $K=2NW-1$, the multitaper estimate errs by 6.3~dB where the spectrum is low, compared with 1.5~dB for the Hann route. On records of 128 samples the errors are 15~dB and 1.7~dB. Dropping the last taper reduces the multitaper error to 2.1~dB. Thomson's adaptive weights \cite{thomson1982,thomson1991} reduce it to 1.7~dB by down-weighting the leaky tapers at frequencies where the spectrum is far below its maximum. At those frequencies the adaptively weighted estimate has $\nu=6.5$ instead of 14 (Fig.~\ref{fig:ar4}). Recent analysis recommends $K=2NW-O(\log NW)$ for the same reason \cite{karnik2022}. Over the whole band the adaptively weighted estimate and the Hann-tapered, smoothed periodogram have the same error, 1.4~dB. The tapered, smoothed periodogram therefore matches the adaptive estimator where the spectrum is low, and has a third fewer degrees of freedom where it is high.

\paragraph{Welch's method and hybrid estimators.} Welch's method with half-overlapping Hann segments, matched in half-power width to the other estimators, has the most degrees of freedom in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} ($\nu=17.6$) and the smallest errors. Its segments are nearly orthogonal, so its ladder is nearly flat. Its disadvantage is the shape of its kernel. The spectral window of a short Hann segment has no flat top, and the base of its main lobe, $4/L$, is almost three times its half-power width. As a result it separates two equal tones only when they are $10.8/N$ apart, compared with $9.0/N$ for the Hann-tapered box of the same half-power width. Bronez found that multitaper estimation outperforms Welch's method when both resolution and leakage are matched \cite{bronez1992}. The two findings are consistent, because giving the Welch kernel sharp edges requires longer segments, and $\nu$ then falls below Thomson's. The routes can also be combined. Riedel, Sidorenko and Thomson found that a few tapers followed by smoothing performed best on plasma data \cite{riedel1994}, and we find the same. Two well-concentrated Slepian tapers ($NW=2$) followed by the box give $\nu=13.3$, a side lobe at $-34$~dB, and errors of 1.3, 1.9 and 1.6~dB.

\section{Recipes for the three routes}
\label{sec:recipe}

The three routes are interchangeable, so the choice among them can be made on practical grounds. In the boxes below, $N$ is the number of samples and $2W$ is the desired resolution, expressed as a full width in cycles per sample (divide by the sampling interval to obtain hertz). We recommend setting $2W$ to about the width of the narrowest feature that must be preserved (Section~\ref{sec:howwide}).

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Exact versions.} These give the same estimate, to machine precision for (a) and (b) and to a fraction of a decibel for (c). The windows in (b) and (c) follow from requiring equality with (a) (identity \eqref{eq:identity}, Appendix~\ref{app:welch}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] \emph{Multitaper.} Compute all $N$ Slepian tapers for $(N,W)$ and their concentrations $\lambda_k$. Average the eigenspectra with weights $\lambda_k/2NW$.
\item[(b)] \emph{Smooth.} Compute the periodogram of the untapered record, zero-padded to at least $2N$ points. Convolve it along frequency with a box of half-width $W$.
\item[(c)] \emph{Average.} Use a sinc window $w_t=2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2Wt)$ many times longer than the record. Place it at every integer offset, including offsets at which it extends beyond the zero-padded ends. Sum the squared transforms and divide by $2NW$.
\end{enumerate}
The estimate that these share contains the leakage of the untapered periodogram.
\end{framed}

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Versions for practical use.} These remove the leakage in three different ways, so they are close but not identical.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a$'$)] \emph{Multitaper.} Keep the $K=2NW-1$ tapers with $\lambda_k\approx1$ and weight them adaptively, or keep $K=2NW-2$ with equal weights. Expect $\nu\approx2K$ where the spectrum is high and about half that where it is far below its maximum.
\item[(b$'$)] \emph{Smooth.} Multiply the record by a Hann window scaled to unit energy. A Bohman window has a higher first side lobe ($-31$ against $-36$~dB) but far skirts that fall faster (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}b), at a further cost in $\nu$. Compute the squared FFT, zero-padded to at least $2N$ points, or more for a finer display grid. Convolve with a box of half-width $W$, or with a Gaussian of standard deviation about $0.8W$, which has a comparable half-power width. Expect a half-power width of about $2W$ once $2W$ exceeds a few $/N$, a first side lobe at $-36$~dB, and $\nu\approx0.55$ to $0.6$ times $2\cdot2NW$. Replacing the Hann window with two Slepian tapers at half the time--bandwidth product nearly doubles $\nu$.
\item[(c$'$)] \emph{Average.} Use Hann-tapered segments of length $L\approx1.4/(2W)$ that overlap by half, and average their periodograms. Expect $\nu$ of about twice the number of segments, a kernel with no flat top whose main lobe has a base of $4/L$, and leakage set by the Hann window of length $L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{framed}

We suggest route (a$'$) when the record is short enough that every degree of freedom matters ($2NW\lesssim6$), when Thomson's F-test for spectral lines is needed, or when coherence and cross-spectra are required, since an orthogonal bank has advantages there that a single taper lacks \cite{walden2000}. Route (b$'$) is preferable when the bandwidth should vary with frequency, which is difficult to arrange with tapers or segments, and for time--frequency displays, where smoothing along both axes is natural. It is also the easiest of the three to interpret. Route (c$'$) suits long records and pipelines that already compute it. At matched half-power width it has the most degrees of freedom and the poorest two-tone resolution. For any route, $\nu$ determines the error bar. The 95\% confidence interval of Section~\ref{sec:periodogram} extends from $-3.3$ to $+5.3$~dB around the estimate for $\nu=8.9$, and from $-2.7$ to $+4.0$~dB for $\nu=14$.

\section{Application to EEG with seizures}
\label{sec:eeg}

\paragraph{Data and settings.} Figs.~\ref{fig:eeg} and \ref{fig:eeg2} apply the three practical estimators to two ten-minute scalp EEG clips that contain focal seizures. Clinicians read spectrograms of recordings like these. The clips are de-identified recordings from a seizure-annotation set (ELROND) drawn from the Brain Data Science Platform. They were sampled at 200~Hz on 20 channels, and one of the authors (M.B.W.) marked the seizure onsets and offsets. For each clip we show the channel with the largest ictal rise in power between 2 and 20~Hz. The features to preserve are the harmonics of the ictal rhythm. On the Hann periodogram at the ictal instant of Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg}, the fundamental is at $7.7$~Hz and the harmonics are $7.4$~Hz apart. Each peak is $0.9$~Hz wide in a 2-second window, which is the resolution of the window and an upper bound on the width of the rhythm itself. In the second clip the harmonic spacing falls to about 4~Hz as the seizure slows. Following the rule of Section~\ref{sec:howwide}, the bandwidth should be well below the harmonic spacing and otherwise as wide as possible. We set $2W=2$~Hz in 2-second windows. The multitaper estimate uses $NW=2$, $K=3$ and equal weights, the smoothed estimate uses a Hann taper with a box of half-width 1~Hz, and Welch's method uses 0.7-second segments.

\paragraph{Results.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg} the ictal rhythm begins near 7.5~Hz with harmonics up to 25~Hz, slows over about a minute, and ends in post-ictal suppression. This descending pattern is often called a chirp and is typical of an evolving seizure. Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg2} contains two such seizures, each with a stack of harmonics that descends from 20~Hz to 3~Hz. These descriptions are based on reading the spectrograms. The unsmoothed periodogram shows the chirps but is very noisy. The multitaper and smoothed-periodogram spectrograms cannot be told apart by inspection, and the spectra at a pre-ictal and an ictal instant (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg}d,\,f) show all three practical estimators resolving the same harmonics. Over all $599$ windows the two spectrograms differ by a median of $1.5$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg}h) and $1.2$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg2}). These differences are smaller than the noise of the estimators, which is $2.5$ and $2.8$~dB for $\nu=6$ and $\nu\approx5$, because the two estimates share most of their noise.

Band powers agree more closely than individual spectral values, because integration over a band averages the noise that distinguishes the estimators (Table~\ref{tab:bandpower}). In Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg} the ictal rise in theta, alpha and beta power is 17 to 18~dB by every estimator, and the estimators agree to within $0.7$~dB. The disagreement with the multitaper estimate in individual windows is about $1$~dB for the Hann route and $0.5$~dB for Welch's method. Conclusions drawn from band powers in these recordings would not depend on the route.

\section{Relation to prior work}
\label{sec:prior}

\paragraph{Established results.} The results assembled here are established, but they are scattered across sources that most users of spectral estimation do not read. Identity \eqref{eq:identity} is Thomson's equation (8.3) \cite{thomson1982}, which takes up half a page of a forty-page paper. The fact that any quadratic estimator is a weighted multitaper estimator, with the eigenvectors of its matrix as tapers, appears in the textbook of Percival and Walden \cite{percival1993}. Riedel and Sidorenko developed it for kernel smoothers \cite{riedel1995} and Walden for multivariate estimators \cite{walden2000}. The degrees-of-freedom formula \eqref{eq:ladder} is Satterthwaite's approximation \cite{satterthwaite1946} applied to this decomposition. Welch showed that averaging the periodograms of a sliding window smooths the periodogram \cite{welch1967}, and Nuttall and Carter gave the general form with combined time and lag weighting \cite{nuttall1982}. Hansson-Sandsten approximated the matrix of the Thomson estimator by that of a Welch estimator, using least squares, for real-time use \cite{hansson2012}. Abreu and Romero proved that the $K$-taper kernel converges to a box and gave the rate \cite{abreu2017}. Karnik, Romberg and Davenport used \eqref{eq:identity} in matrix form in a fast multitaper algorithm \cite{karnik2022}. Riedel, Sidorenko and Thomson compared multitaper estimates with smoothed tapered periodograms \cite{riedel1994}, and Bronez compared them with Welch's method \cite{bronez1992}. Harris catalogued the properties of windows \cite{harris1978}. Most of this literature is written in terms of eigenvalue problems, error rates and matrix algorithms. The texts from which neuroscientists learn spectral estimation \cite{mitra1999,bokil2010,babadi2014,prerau2017,kay1988} present the three methods separately.

\paragraph{Contributions of this paper.} We combine these results in a single description, the bank of tapers, and work out its practical consequences. The sliding-window form of the identity relates the averaging route exactly to the other two, and identifies the Slepian sequences as the principal components of a sliding sinc window. The eigen-weight ladder explains a standard degrees-of-freedom formula in terms that apply to all three routes, since the variance cost of a single taper corresponds to the slope of its ladder. Tables~\ref{tab:ensemble} and \ref{tab:dof} list the cost of each route and the conditions under which each performs best, including the adaptively weighted estimate. The bandwidth rule and the recipes allow the parameters to be chosen from the features of the data. Bruns showed in a similar way that Fourier, Hilbert and wavelet analyses of neural signals are one method \cite{bruns2004}. The bank-of-tapers description covers those methods as well. A Morlet scalogram, for example, is a single-taper periodogram whose Gaussian taper shortens as frequency increases.

\section{Limitations}
The identity is exact only for the eigenvalue-weighted estimate with all $N$ tapers, and its sliding-window form only in the limit of a long window. The versions used in practice are close but not equal, and we have quantified the differences without removing them.

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} uses a single process, a fourth-order autoregression with two peaks and a range of 65~dB. The estimators are compared at matched design bandwidth and not at matched $\nu$. The median error over the whole band is dominated by the region of low power, where leakage is largest. For this reason the table also reports the error at the peaks, where the ranking of the estimators is reversed. Other spectra, spectral lines and very short records will rank the routes differently. The adaptive weights are those of Thomson's original iteration and omit the later jackknife and quadratic-inverse refinements \cite{thomson1991}.

The kernels, degrees of freedom and leakage values describe the estimators for a stationary Gaussian process and were computed on a finite FFT grid. The spectrograms assume stationarity within each 2-second window, an assumption that fails at the onset of a seizure. The EEG evidence consists of two clips with one channel each, analyzed with equal-weight multitaper estimates. We did not consider coherence or cross-spectra, inference beyond the $\chi^2_\nu$ interval, or data-driven bandwidth selection.

\section{Conclusion}
Averaging periodograms, smoothing a periodogram and multitaper estimation are the same estimator, a bank of tapers with weights that can be built from one window in three ways. When all Slepian tapers are used with their eigenvalues as weights, the multitaper estimate equals a smoothed periodogram exactly, and in the limit of a long window it equals an average of sliding-window periodograms. The estimator used in practice is this estimate with its leaky components removed. This description expresses the multitaper parameters in terms that users of the other two methods already know. The taper controls the skirts of the kernel, and the box width or segment length controls the width of its main lobe. The bandwidth is chosen by balancing blur against noise. A tapered and smoothed periodogram controls leakage as well as an adaptively weighted multitaper estimate and has a third fewer degrees of freedom. Without a taper, a smoothed or averaged periodogram is biased by leakage wherever the spectrum is low. For most EEG applications any of the three routes is adequate, and the choice can be made for convenience.

\section{Proof of the identity \eqref{eq:identity}}
\label{app:proof}
Let $A$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with entries $A_{tt'}=\sin(2\pi W(t-t'))/(\pi(t-t'))$ and $A_{tt}=2W$. The Slepian sequences are its eigenvectors, $Av_k=\lambda_kv_k$, $\|v_k\|=1$ \cite{slepian1978}, so $A=\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ and $\sum_k\lambda_k=\operatorname{tr}A=2NW$. With $x_f$ the modulated data, $\hat S_k(f)=|v_k^{\!\top}x_f|^2$, and
\begin{align}
\sum_{k}\lambda_k\hat S_k(f)
&=x_f^{*}\Bigl(\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}\Bigr)x_f
=\sum_{t,t'}A_{tt'}\,x_tx_{t'}\,e^{-i2\pi f(t-t')}
=\sum_{\tau=-(N-1)}^{N-1}h_\tau\,\hat s_\tau\,e^{-i2\pi f\tau},
\end{align}
with $h_\tau=\sin(2\pi W\tau)/(\pi\tau)$ and $\hat s_\tau=\sum_tx_tx_{t+\tau}$, collecting terms by lag. Now $\hat s_\tau$ is the inverse transform of $N\hat I(f)$ and $h_\tau$ the inverse transform of the indicator of $[-W,W]$; a product of lag sequences transforms to a convolution in frequency, so the sum equals $\int_{f-W}^{f+W}N\hat I(f')\dd f'$. Divide by $2NW$. $\square$

\section{Kernels, banks and variance of quadratic estimators}
\label{app:quadratic}
Every estimator in this paper is $\hat S(f)=x_f^{*}Qx_f$ with $Q$ real symmetric positive semidefinite and independent of $f$ \cite{percival1993,riedel1995}: $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ for a multitaper estimate; $Q_{tt'}=w_tg_{t-t'}w_{t'}$ for a periodogram tapered with $w$ and smoothed with the lag window $g$ (the inverse transform of the kernel); $Q=\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}/\sum_s\|u_s\|^2$ for averaged periodograms of a window placed at offsets $s$.

\emph{Bank.} $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ with orthonormal $v_k$ and $c_k\ge0$, so every $Q$ is a multitaper estimate \eqref{eq:bank}.

\emph{Kernel.} $\E[\hat S(f)]=\int H(f-f')S(f')\dd f'$ with $H$ the transform of the lag sums $q_\tau=\sum_tQ_{t,t+\tau}$; equivalently $H=\sum_kc_k|V_k|^2$. For a tapered, smoothed periodogram $H=|\mathcal{W}|^2*G$; for averaged periodograms $H=|\mathcal{W}_L|^2$ whatever the overlap; for $Q=A$, $H=B_W*F$, which is why two rows of Table~\ref{tab:dof} coincide.

\emph{Degrees of freedom.} For unit white Gaussian noise, $\hat S(f)$ has mean $\operatorname{tr}Q_f$ and variance $2\operatorname{tr}Q_f^2$, with $Q_f$ the real part of $Q$ conjugated by the modulation, so $\nu=(\operatorname{tr}Q_f)^2/\operatorname{tr}Q_f^2$, the chi-square approximation of Satterthwaite \cite{satterthwaite1946}. At an interior frequency each modulated taper is orthogonal to its conjugate, each term of the bank is an independent $\chi^2_2$, and $\nu=2(\sum c_k)^2/\sum c_k^2$, equation~\eqref{eq:ladder}. The two formulas agree to within 3\% for the three estimators tested (multitaper, Hann-then-box and Welch); Table~\ref{tab:dof} uses the first, and Monte Carlo on white noise (3000 draws) agrees with it to within 15\% for the same three (repository tests).

\emph{Resolution and leakage.} Resolution is the half-power full width of $H$; leakage is the fraction of $H$ outside a stated band; the peak side lobe is the maximum of $H$ beyond $1.5\times$ the half-power half-width. A practitioner's convolution of a zero-padded periodogram with a kernel on the same grid is exactly the lag-window estimate above once the padding reaches $2N$ points, because the periodogram's lag sequence vanishes beyond $|\tau|=N-1$.

\section{Averaging over a sliding window is smoothing}
\label{app:welch}

\subsection*{C.1 Continuous time: the limit of all overlaps}
Let $x(t)$ be the record, zero outside $[0,T]$, with transform $X(\omega)$, and $w$ a real symmetric window with transform $W$. Windowing at shift $\tau$ gives $s_\tau(t)=x(t)w(t-\tau)$, with transform $S_\tau(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int X(\omega')W(\omega-\omega')e^{-i(\omega-\omega')\tau}\dd\omega'$. Welch's method with shifts $n\Delta$ averages $|S_{n\Delta}(\omega)|^2$ over $n$. Let the overlap grow without bound, $\Delta\to0$: the average becomes a Riemann sum for an integral over the shift, which can be evaluated in closed form,
\begin{align}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|S_\tau(\omega)|^2\dd\tau
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint X(\omega')X^{*}(\omega'')W(\omega-\omega')W^{*}(\omega-\omega'')\Bigl[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\,i(\omega'-\omega'')\tau}\dd\tau\Bigr]\dd\omega'\dd\omega''\notag\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int|X(\omega')|^2\,|W(\omega-\omega')|^2\dd\omega'=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl(|W|^2*|X|^2\bigr)(\omega),
\label{eq:continuous}
\end{align}
because the bracket is $2\pi\delta(\omega'-\omega'')$. The sum of the periodograms of all shifted windows is the raw periodogram smoothed with the window's own spectral window; dividing by $T\!\int\!w^2$, the trace of the quadratic form, makes the estimate unbiased for white noise. The shift integral must run over every $\tau$ at which the window touches the record, including shifts at which it extends beyond the ends. These shifts are needed to produce the delta function, and restricting $\tau$ to $[0,T]$ leaves an edge term that vanishes only when $T$ is much longer than the window. For a Gaussian window of standard deviation $\sigma_t$ the kernel is Gaussian with standard deviation $1/(2\sqrt2\,\pi\sigma_t)$ cycles per unit time, so sliding a Gaussian window is equivalent to smoothing with a Gaussian. This calculation, with a Gaussian window, was the content of an unpublished 2001 note by one of us, written before the connection to multitaper estimation was known; the result is Welch's \cite{welch1967} and is contained in Nuttall and Carter's framework \cite{nuttall1982}.

\subsection*{C.2 Discrete time and a finite record}
In discrete time the shift is an integer, and the sum over all shifts gives the result exactly, with no limit required. Let $w$ have length $L$ and $u_s$ be the length-$N$ vector holding $w$ at offset $s$, with the parts of $w$ outside the record discarded. Averaging over offsets gives $Q_{tt'}=\sum_sw[t-s]w[t'-s]$. If the offsets run over every integer for which the window touches the record, $s=-(L-1),\dots,N-1$, the sum equals the autocorrelation $r_w(t-t')$ for every pair inside the record, so $Q$ is Toeplitz and the estimator is the lag-window estimate of the untapered periodogram with lag window $r_w$ and kernel $|\mathcal{W}(f)|^2$. This is exact for a finite record; with a larger step or without overhang it holds approximately in the interior. The equivalence is for the \emph{untapered} periodogram: a tapered periodogram that is then smoothed has $Q_{tt'}=w_tg_{t-t'}w_{t'}$, which is not Toeplitz and is not the average of any single sliding window, though it is a time-and-lag weighting in the sense of \cite{nuttall1982}. For the sinc window, $r_w(\tau)\to2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2W\tau)$ as $L\to\infty$, the lag sequence of $A$; the relative error in $Q$ falls as $1/L$ because the truncated sinc's autocorrelation differs from the sinc by a term of that order. Hence \eqref{eq:sliding}, and the Slepian sequences are the principal components of the shifted sinc windows.
