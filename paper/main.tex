\begin{abstract}
\textit{Objective:} The parameters of multitaper spectral estimation have no counterpart in the two methods most users learn first, averaging the periodograms of segments and smoothing one periodogram across frequency. \textit{Methods:} We write each estimator as a bank of tapers with weights and compare kernels and degrees of freedom. We score them on simulated spectra, at a fixed bandwidth and with each tuned to its best setting, and on scalp EEG containing seizures. \textit{Results:} The three methods are one estimator, built from a single window by shifting it in time, shifting it in frequency, or taking orthogonal tapers. The multitaper estimate with all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, equals the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$ and, for a long window, the average of periodograms of a sliding sinc window. Where the spectrum is low, the untapered smoothed periodogram errs by 14~dB, the default multitaper estimate by 6~dB, and a tapered smoothed periodogram by 1 to 1.4~dB. A Hann taper costs a third of the multitaper estimate's degrees of freedom and a 25\% cosine taper none. With each route tuned, the smoothed periodogram is at least as accurate as the multitaper estimate. On EEG the routes agree to a median of 0.7 to 1.5~dB. \textit{Conclusion:} A lightly tapered, smoothed periodogram matches an adaptively weighted multitaper estimate in leakage and accuracy. \textit{Significance:} Users can choose the parameters of a spectral estimate from the features of their data and use whichever route their pipeline already computes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{hree} families of spectral estimator are in common use in electrophysiology. The first averages periodograms. The record is cut into segments, a periodogram is computed for each, and the periodograms are averaged. This is Bartlett's method \cite{bartlett1948}, or Welch's \cite{welch1967} when the segments are tapered and overlap, and textbooks such as Kay's \cite{kay1988} present it first. The second smooths a periodogram. One periodogram of the whole record is convolved across frequency with a box (Daniell \cite{daniell1946}) or a Gaussian. This is the Blackman--Tukey estimator \cite{blackman1958}, and many analysis pipelines apply it to spectrograms without naming it. The third is Thomson's multitaper method \cite{thomson1982}, which two tutorials for neuroscientists \cite{babadi2014,prerau2017} recommend because it controls leakage, reduces variance, and is optimal in a defined sense. Its two parameters, however, have no counterpart in the first two methods. A user who knows how to choose a segment length or a smoothing width has no obvious way to decide what time--bandwidth product to use, or whether to keep seven tapers or six. The tapers themselves, Slepian's discrete prolate spheroidal sequences \cite{slepian1978}, are also unfamiliar to most users.

We show that the three methods are the same estimator (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract}). Each is a quadratic form in the data, and a quadratic form can be written as a bank of tapers with weights. Averaging periodograms builds the bank by shifting one window along the record. Smoothing builds it by shifting one taper along the frequency axis. Thomson's method builds it from orthogonal tapers. Each bank has an expected-value kernel and an equivalent number of degrees of freedom, so the three methods can be compared on the same terms. One of the correspondences is exact. The multitaper estimate with all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, equals the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$. This is equation (8.3) of Thomson's paper \cite{thomson1982}, and neither tutorial mentions it. Averaging the periodograms of a sliding window is itself a form of smoothing \cite{welch1967,nuttall1982}, so sliding a sinc window across the record produces the same estimate, and the Slepian tapers are the principal components of that sliding window.

These results have three practical consequences, which we measured over 300 realizations of a spectrum spanning 65~dB. Leakage survives smoothing and averaging. Where the spectrum is low, a box-smoothed periodogram errs by 14~dB without a taper and by 1.4~dB with one. A Hann taper has a third fewer degrees of freedom than the multitaper estimate at the same bandwidth ($\nu=8.9$ against $14.0$), which raises the error at the spectral peaks from 1.8 to 2.3~dB. A cosine taper applied only to the ends of the record avoids this loss ($\nu=14.8$ with the same box, and $16.7$ with a parabola of the same half-power width, whose base is wider). With default settings the multitaper estimate errs by 6~dB where the spectrum is low, and by 15~dB on records of 128 samples, while a tapered, smoothed periodogram errs by 1.0 to 1.4~dB. Adaptive weights remove this difference. When every route is tuned to its best setting, a tapered, smoothed periodogram is at least as accurate as the multitaper estimate in each of four test cases.

The paper makes five contributions. We prove that averaging periodograms, smoothing a periodogram and multitaper estimation are one estimator (Section~\ref{sec:one}). We derive the sliding-window form of Thomson's identity and show that the Slepian sequences are the principal components of a sinc window slid across the record (Section~\ref{sec:sliding}, Appendix~\ref{app:welch}). We show that the variance cost of a single taper belongs to the taper and not to smoothing, that the best smoothing kernel is a parabola, and that for white noise the smoothed periodogram has the smallest variance of all estimators with a given kernel (Sections~\ref{sec:shape} and \ref{sec:beforeafter}). We compute exact kernels, leakage and degrees of freedom for eleven estimators at matched bandwidth, together with their errors over an ensemble (Tables~\ref{tab:ensemble} and \ref{tab:dof}). We give a recipe for each route and a rule for choosing its bandwidth (Sections~\ref{sec:howwide} and \ref{sec:recipe}), and we test both on scalp EEG containing focal seizures (Section~\ref{sec:eeg}).

The method is the same throughout. We write each estimator as a quadratic form, diagonalize it, and compare estimators through their kernels and eigen-weight ladders. Sections~\ref{sec:periodogram} and \ref{sec:fixes} introduce the kernel picture and the three classical remedies. Section~\ref{sec:mt} presents multitaper estimation in the usual way. Section~\ref{sec:one} shows that the three methods are one and quantifies how the versions used in practice differ. Sections~\ref{sec:recipe} and \ref{sec:eeg} give the recipes and the EEG demonstration. Proofs are in the appendices.


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The Periodogram and the Kernel Picture}
\label{sec:periodogram}
Every estimator in this paper returns the true spectrum blurred by a kernel, plus noise. For the periodogram the kernel is Fej\'er's and the noise is at its largest. Let $x_0,\dots,x_{N-1}$ be $N$ samples of a stationary signal. We take the sampling interval as the unit of time, so that frequency $f$ is in cycles per sample and runs from $-\tfrac12$ to $\tfrac12$. The periodogram is
\begin{equation}
\hat I(f) = \frac{1}{N}\Bigl|\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} x_t\, e^{-i2\pi f t}\Bigr|^2 ,
\label{eq:periodogram}
\end{equation}
the squared magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform. Zero-padding before the fast Fourier transform (FFT) evaluates it on as fine a grid as needed. It has two well-known defects, both visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}(a,\,e).

\paragraph{Noise} At any one frequency, the periodogram of a Gaussian signal is distributed as the true power spectral density (PSD) multiplied by a chi-square variable with two degrees of freedom, divided by two. Its standard deviation therefore equals its mean. A longer record does not reduce the noise, because it adds frequency points that are each as noisy as before.

\paragraph{Leakage} The expected value of the periodogram is the true PSD $S$ convolved with a kernel,
\begin{equation}
\E[\hat I(f)] = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} F(f-f')\,S(f')\dd f' ,
\label{eq:fejer}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
F(f)=\frac{1}{N}\biggl(\frac{\sin N\pi f}{\sin \pi f}\biggr)^{2}
\label{eq:fejerkernel}
\end{equation}
is Fej\'er's kernel, the squared Fourier transform of the rectangular window that truncated the record. $F$ has a main lobe about $2/N$ wide and side lobes that decay only as $1/f^2$. Neural spectra vary by tens of decibels across the band. The tails of $F$ then carry power from the strong part of the spectrum into the weak part, where the estimate is set by leakage instead of by the signal.

\paragraph{The kernel picture} Equation~\eqref{eq:fejer} generalizes. Every estimator in this paper satisfies
\begin{equation}
\E[\hat S(f)] = \int H(f-f')\,S(f')\dd f' ,
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the \emph{expected-value kernel} of the estimator, and the estimate fluctuates around this expected value. Three quantities characterize an estimator. Its resolution is the width of the main lobe of $H$. Its leakage is the height of the skirts of $H$. Its variance is reported as an equivalent number of degrees of freedom $\nu$, meaning that the estimate behaves like $S\,\chi^2_\nu/\nu$. The relative standard error is then $\sqrt{2/\nu}$, or about $4.3\sqrt{2/\nu}$~dB, and an approximate 95\% confidence interval runs from $\nu\hat S/\chi^2_{\nu,0.975}$ to $\nu\hat S/\chi^2_{\nu,0.025}$. The periodogram has the narrowest main lobe possible, high leakage, and $\nu=2$. Each remedy below improves one of the three quantities at the expense of another. Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic} gives the formulas used to compute the kernel and $\nu$ of each estimator exactly, and all numbers quoted below come from them.


\subsection{Three Classical Remedies}
\label{sec:fixes}
\subsubsection{Averaging the periodograms of segments}
\label{sec:averaging}
Bartlett's remedy for the noise \cite{bartlett1948} is to cut the record into $M$ segments of length $L$, compute a periodogram of each, and average them. Segments that do not overlap give independent estimates with $\nu=2$ each, so the average has $\nu\approx2M$. Resolution is lost in exchange. The kernel of each segment is the Fej\'er kernel of length $L$, whose main lobe is $2/L=2M/N$ wide, and averaging does not narrow it. Welch \cite{welch1967} tapers each segment to control leakage and overlaps the segments by half their length to recover the data lost at the tapered ends. Hann segments at 50\% overlap are nearly uncorrelated \cite{welch1967,percival1993}, so $\nu$ is again close to twice the number of segments (13.4 for seven segments in Table~\ref{tab:dof}). Whatever the overlap, the kernel of the estimator is the spectral window of the segment taper, $|\mathcal{W}_L(f)|^2$. The segment length therefore sets the bandwidth, and using more and shorter segments raises $\nu$ and widens the kernel.

\subsubsection{Smoothing one periodogram}
\label{sec:smoothing}
The second remedy computes one periodogram of the whole record and averages it over a window of frequencies. The window may be a box of half-width $W$ \cite{daniell1946}, a Gaussian, or any non-negative kernel $G$. Because expectation commutes with averaging, the kernel of the smoothed estimate is the kernel of the unsmoothed one convolved with the smoothing window, $H=G*F$. The main lobe widens to about $2W$, which the user sets directly. The noise falls, because periodogram values $1/N$ apart are nearly independent and a band of width $2W$ pools about $2NW$ of them, giving $\nu\approx2\cdot2NW$. The leakage does not change, because convolution with a non-negative window redistributes the skirts and cannot cancel them. This is the Blackman--Tukey estimator \cite{blackman1958} computed in the frequency domain. Convolving a spectrogram with a Gaussian along the frequency axis is the same operation. Section~\ref{sec:sliding} shows that smoothing and averaging are the same remedy.

\subsubsection{Tapering}
\label{sec:tapering}
Averaging and smoothing do not reduce leakage, which is why both are usually applied to tapered periodograms. A taper is a window $w_t$ that falls smoothly to zero at both ends of the record, and the data are multiplied by it before the transform. The kernel becomes $|\mathcal{W}(f)|^2$, the squared transform of the taper. We use a cosine (Tukey) taper, which is flat over the middle of the record and falls to zero as half a cosine at each end (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}a). With one eighth of the record tapered at each end, which we call a 25\% cosine taper, the tails of the kernel decay as $1/f^6$ instead of $1/f^2$ beyond about $8/N$. They are 22~dB below those of the untapered kernel at $16/N$ and 39~dB below at $40/N$, and the main lobe is 15\% wider (Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}f). The Hann window tapers the whole record. Its tails are a further 25~dB lower and its main lobe is 60\% wider than that of the untapered kernel. Tapering does not reduce the variance, and a single tapered periodogram still has $\nu=2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:motif}b). A taper also discards data, because the samples near the ends receive little weight. After smoothing, $\nu$ is divided by $N\sum_tw_t^4/(\sum_tw_t^2)^2$, which is $1.94$ for the Hann window and $1.15$ for the 25\% cosine taper. The taper sets how fast the skirts fall. The smoothing window, or the segment length, sets the width of the main lobe.

\subsubsection{Choosing the bandwidth}
\label{sec:howwide}
Each remedy requires a bandwidth, set by the segment length, by the half-width $W$, or by the time--bandwidth product of Section~\ref{sec:mt}. Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} shows how the choice affects a smoothed periodogram. A wider kernel pools more periodogram ordinates, so the noise falls as $4.3\sqrt{2/\nu}$~dB with $\nu$ roughly proportional to $NW$ (blue curve). A wider kernel also blurs the spectrum over a width $2W$. A peak narrower than $2W$ is lowered and broadened, and a valley narrower than $2W$ is partly filled. This bias is zero for a flat spectrum and grows with the curvature of $S$ on the scale of the kernel (orange curve). Noise and bias add in quadrature, and their total has a broad minimum where the two are comparable.

The position of the minimum depends on the narrowest feature that must be preserved. The peaks in Fig.~\ref{fig:motif} are $7/N$ wide, so a kernel with $2W\approx7/N$, that is $W=4/N$, is the widest that does not flatten them. With this kernel the estimate has $\nu=16.7$ and follows the true spectrum to within its own noise of about $\pm1.5$~dB. A narrower kernel gives a noisier estimate than necessary ($\nu=2$, error 6~dB). A kernel wider than the separation of the peaks merges them ($W=24/N$, $\nu=98$), and although the estimate looks clean, its error over the peak region is 5.5~dB. The optimum also depends on the region that is scored. It is $W\approx6/N$ over the whole band, most of which has no features, and $W\approx4/N$ over the peaks. For real data the optimum is therefore not known in advance. We suggest the rule that Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} illustrates. Identify the narrowest feature of interest, for example an alpha peak one to two hertz wide, and set $2W$ to about its width. Data-driven choices of bandwidth exist for smoothed periodograms \cite{wahba1980,hurvich1985} and for multitaper estimates \cite{haley2017}. They optimize a criterion over the whole band, so the same caveat applies.

\subsubsection{The shape of the smoothing kernel}
\label{sec:shape}
The best shape for the kernel can be derived to leading order, for a tapered periodogram at a frequency away from 0 and $\tfrac12$, where the spectrum is smooth on the scale of the kernel and the kernel is much wider than $1/N$. There a non-negative kernel $G$ of unit area gives the smoothed periodogram a bias of about $\tfrac12S''(f)\int u^2G(u)\dd u$ and a relative variance of about $(c_w/N)\int G^2(u)\dd u$. The constant $c_w=N\sum_tw_t^4/(\sum_tw_t^2)^2$ is the cost of the taper (Section~\ref{sec:tapering}). Each shape has a width at which its mean square error is smallest, and that smallest error is proportional to $(\int u^2G)^{2/5}(\int G^2)^{4/5}$. Among non-negative kernels this quantity is least for the parabola
\begin{equation}
G(u)=\frac{3}{4b}\Bigl(1-\frac{u^2}{b^2}\Bigr),\qquad|u|\le b,
\label{eq:parabola}
\end{equation}
which Priestley derived for spectral estimation \cite{priestley1962} and Epanechnikov for density estimation \cite{epanechnikov1969}. Its lag window, $3(\sin a-a\cos a)/a^3$ with $a=2\pi b\tau$, is the quadratic-spectral kernel of Andrews \cite[Theorem~2]{andrews1991}. Its only parameter is the half-width $b$. Its half-power width is $\sqrt2\,b$, and the smoothed estimate has $\nu\approx(10/3)Nb/c_w$. The shape matters little. Asymptotically, at their best widths, the box and the Gaussian have root-mean-square errors 3\% and 2\% larger than the parabola. Kernels with negative lobes can do better, and at a fixed width the parabola is not optimal. The width matters more. The best half-width is
\begin{equation}
b_{\mathrm{opt}}=\Bigl(\frac{15\,c_w}{N}\Bigr)^{1/5}\,\Bigl|\frac{S(f)}{S''(f)}\Bigr|^{2/5}.
\label{eq:bopt}
\end{equation}
It depends on the curvature of the spectrum, which changes from one frequency to the next and is not known in advance, and only weakly on the length of the record. For the alpha peak of the EEG-like spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}, which is 1.4~Hz wide, \eqref{eq:bopt} gives a half-power width of 1.5~Hz in a 2-second record. The width that minimizes the exact mean square error is 1.9~Hz, because the peak is too narrow for the quadratic approximation. Both agree with the rule of setting the width of the kernel to about the width of the narrowest feature. Appendix~\ref{app:variational} derives \eqref{eq:parabola} and \eqref{eq:bopt} by the calculus of variations.

\subsubsection{Time resolution and the time--bandwidth product}
\label{sec:timefreq}
A spectrogram requires a second choice, the length $T$ of the window. Events closer together than $T$ are merged in time, and peaks closer together than the half-power width $\Delta f$ of the kernel are merged in frequency. The two resolutions fix the noise, because
\begin{equation}
\nu\approx2\,T\,\Delta f,
\label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
with $T$ in seconds and $\Delta f$ in hertz. For windows of 1 to 8~s and design bandwidths of 1 to 4~Hz, the ratio of $\nu$ to $2T\Delta f$ is between $0.91$ and $0.98$ for the multitaper estimate with $K=2NW-1$ and between $1.03$ and $1.15$ for recipe (b$'$). A user therefore chooses two of the three quantities, time resolution, frequency resolution and noise, and the third follows, by whichever route the estimate is computed. In EEG the choice is constrained by physiology. Sleep spindles last 0.5 to 2~s and lie between 11 and 16~Hz, and epileptiform discharges last less than a second. Windows of 1 to 2~s and kernels 1 to 2~Hz wide leave $\nu$ between 2 and 8. In this range the time--bandwidth product $NW=T\Delta f/2$ is 1 or 2, and the multitaper estimate has one to three tapers.

\subsection{Multitaper Estimation}
\label{sec:mt}
Thomson's method \cite{thomson1982} achieves low leakage and low variance together, without shortening the record, by using several orthogonal tapers. The user chooses a half-bandwidth $W$. The tapers are the sequences of length $N$ that are mutually orthogonal and whose transforms are most concentrated in $[-W,W]$. These are Slepian's sequences \cite{slepian1978}. There are $N$ of them, $v_0,\dots,v_{N-1}$, ordered by their concentrations $1>\lambda_0>\lambda_1>\dots>0$, where $\lambda_k$ is the fraction of the spectral energy of the $k$th taper that lies inside the band. For $NW=4$ the first six concentrations exceed $0.99$, the seventh is $\lambda_6=0.94$, and the rest fall rapidly toward zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}d). Each taper gives an eigenspectrum $\hat S_k(f)=|\sum_t v_{k,t}x_te^{-i2\pi ft}|^2$, and the estimate is the average of the first $K=2NW-1$ eigenspectra,
\begin{equation}
\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)=\frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\hat S_k(f).
\label{eq:mt}
\end{equation}

The method works because its kernel is close to a box. Each taper has low leakage, so each eigenspectrum has little bias. The tapers are orthogonal, so the eigenspectra are nearly uncorrelated and the average of $K$ of them has $\nu\approx2K$. The kernel of \eqref{eq:mt} is the average of the $K$ spectral windows $|U_k|^2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}b). The zeroth window is a single bump at the center of the band, the first has two bumps on either side of the center, and the second has three. Each window fills gaps left by the previous ones, and their sum is nearly flat over $[-W,W]$ and nearly zero outside it (Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}c). Thomson's optimality result is that no other set of $K$ orthogonal tapers places more energy inside the band.

\subsection{The Three Methods as One Estimator}
\label{sec:one}

\subsubsection{Every estimator is a bank of tapers}
\label{sec:banks}
Every estimator in this paper is a quadratic form in the data, and every quadratic form can be written as a bank of orthogonal tapers with weights. Let $x_f$ denote the data modulated to frequency $f$, with entries $x_te^{-i2\pi ft}$. Then $\hat S(f)=x_f^{*}Qx_f$ for a fixed real symmetric matrix $Q$ \cite{percival1993,riedel1995}. The eigendecomposition $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$, with orthonormal $v_k$, gives
\begin{equation}
\hat S(f)=\sum_k c_k\,\bigl|v_k^{\!\top}x_f\bigr|^2 ,
\label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
which has the form of a multitaper estimate with tapers $v_k$ and weights $c_k$. The three methods differ in how they construct the bank (Fig.~\ref{fig:banks}). Averaging periodograms places one window at $M$ offsets and adds the squared transforms with equal weight, so that $Q=\frac1M\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}$. The bank consists of \emph{time-shifted} copies of one window, which are orthogonal only if the segments do not overlap. Smoothing a tapered periodogram with a kernel $G$ gives $\int G(f')\,|(w\odot e_{f'})^{\!\top}x_f|^2\dd f'$. Here the bank consists of \emph{frequency-shifted} copies of one taper, weighted by the kernel. Thomson's method starts from an orthogonal bank.

The kernel and the degrees of freedom both follow from the bank. The kernel is $H=\sum_kc_k|V_k|^2$. At an interior frequency the degrees of freedom are
\begin{equation}
\nu=\frac{2\bigl(\sum_kc_k\bigr)^2}{\sum_kc_k^2},
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
which is Satterthwaite's approximation \cite{satterthwaite1946} applied to the bank and agrees with the exact value to within 3\% for the estimators we tested (Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic}). The value is $2K$ for $K$ equal weights and smaller when the weights are unequal. We call the ordered weights $c_0\ge c_1\ge\dots$ the \emph{eigen-weight ladder}. Thomson's ladder is flat by construction. The ladder of a smoothed single-taper periodogram slopes downward, because the modulated copies of one taper overlap. How much this costs depends on the taper and on the kernel. With a Hann window and a box the ladder is steep and short ($\nu=8.9$). With a 25\% cosine taper and a parabola it slopes gently over more steps ($\nu=16.7$; Fig.~\ref{fig:banks}f). The ladder of Welch's method with half-overlapping Hann segments is nearly flat, and the cost of that method appears in the shape of its kernel.

\subsubsection{Thomson's identity}
\label{sec:identity}
Suppose that all $N$ tapers are kept and each eigenspectrum is weighted by its concentration. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\frac{1}{2NW}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\lambda_k\,\hat S_k(f)\;=\;\frac{1}{2W}\int_{f-W}^{f+W}\hat I(f')\dd f'\;}
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The left side is the eigenvalue-weighted average of all $N$ eigenspectra, whose weights sum to $2NW$. The right side is the periodogram averaged over a box of half-width $W$. This is Thomson's equation (8.3) \cite{thomson1982}. It holds for each record individually as well as in expectation, and we confirmed it to machine precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract}).

The proof is brief. The Slepian sequences are the eigenvectors of the $N\times N$ matrix $A$ with entries $\sin(2\pi W(t-t'))/(\pi(t-t'))$, and the $\lambda_k$ are its eigenvalues. The entries depend only on the lag $t-t'$, and as a function of lag they form a sinc, which is the inverse transform of the box. The matrix $A$ therefore represents the box kernel, and $A=\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ is its decomposition into orthogonal components. Evaluating $x_f^{*}Ax_f$ through the eigenvectors gives the left side of \eqref{eq:identity}, and evaluating it through the lags gives the right side (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}). In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:banks}, the Slepian tapers are the eigen-tapers of the box-smoothed periodogram. Their concentrations form its ladder, which is flat near one for $k<2NW$ and then falls to zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:banks}c).


\subsubsection{Averaging as smoothing}
\label{sec:sliding}
The matrix $A$ also arises from averaging. Consider a window $w$ that slides across the record one sample at a time and is allowed to extend beyond both ends of the zero-padded record. The squared transforms of the windowed data are summed over all positions. Appendix~\ref{app:welch} shows that the matrix $Q$ of this estimator has entries $r_w(t-t')$, where $r_w$ is the autocorrelation of the window. Averaging the periodograms of a sliding window is therefore exactly the same as smoothing the untapered periodogram with $|\mathcal{W}|^2$. For a sinc window $w_t=2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2Wt)$ the autocorrelation is $2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2W\tau)$, which is the lag sequence of $A$, so that
\begin{equation}
\sum_s\bigl|u_s^{\!\top}x_f\bigr|^2=x_f^{*}Ax_f=\sum_k\lambda_k\hat S_k(f),
\label{eq:sliding}
\end{equation}
where $u_s$ is the sinc window at offset $s$. The equality is exact for an infinitely long window. For a window of length $L$ the relative error in $Q$ is 3.5\% at $L=2N$, 1.5\% at $4N$ and 0.17\% at $32N$, and it decreases as $1/L$ once $L$ exceeds $2N$ (Appendix~\ref{app:welch}). The matrix $A=\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}$ is the scatter matrix of the shifted windows. Its eigenvectors, the Slepian sequences, are therefore the principal components of the sliding sinc window, and Thomson's estimator retains the leading components.


\subsubsection{The estimators used in practice}
\label{sec:everyday}
The standard estimate \eqref{eq:mt} differs from the left side of \eqref{eq:identity} in two ways. It uses equal weights, which changes little because $\lambda_k\approx1$ for $k<K$. It also stops at $K=2NW-1$ tapers, and this is the difference that matters. Rearranging \eqref{eq:identity} gives
\begin{multline}
\frac{K}{2NW}\,\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)\approx\underbrace{\frac{1}{2W}\int_{f-W}^{f+W}\hat I(f')\dd f'}_{\text{box-smoothed periodogram}}\\
-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2NW}\sum_{k\ge K}\lambda_k\hat S_k(f)}_{\text{leaky remainder}} .
\label{eq:corollary}
\end{multline}
The factor $K/2NW$, which is $7/8$ for $NW=4$, arises because the standard estimate averages over $K$ tapers while the weights in \eqref{eq:identity} sum to $2NW$. The eigenspectra that are dropped belong to tapers whose energy lies almost entirely outside $[-W,W]$, so each contributes at frequency $f$ only power that has leaked in from other frequencies. The multitaper estimate is thus the box-smoothed periodogram, or equivalently the sliding-sinc average, with this leakage subtracted. Thomson noted that the two agree ``only for white spectra'' \cite{thomson1982}.

\subsubsection{Smoothing before squaring, or after}
\label{sec:beforeafter}
Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency. The transform of the tapered record $v_k(t)x_t$ is therefore the transform $X$ of the record convolved with the transform $V_k$ of the taper, and the multitaper estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)=\frac1K\sum_{k<K}\Bigl|\int V_k(f-f')\,X(f')\dd f'\Bigr|^2 .
\label{eq:before}
\end{equation}
It can be computed from one Fourier transform of the untapered record. It smooths the complex transform in $K$ ways, squares each result, and averages. A smoothed periodogram performs these operations in the other order. It squares first and smooths afterwards,
\begin{equation}
\hat S_{\mathrm{sm}}(f)=\int G(f-f')\,|X(f')|^2\dd f' .
\label{eq:after}
\end{equation}
The two orders differ because squaring discards the phase of $X$. The square in \eqref{eq:before} expands into a double integral over products $X(f')X^{*}(f'')$ of the transform at pairs of frequencies, and \eqref{eq:after} keeps only the terms with $f'=f''$. For a stationary process the products at different frequencies are close to zero on average and are not zero in any one record. This is why the two estimators can have the same expected value, and why their estimates of one record differ.

The two parts can be written out. Let $X_j=X(f_j)$ be the transform at the Fourier frequencies $f_j=j/N$, and $\hat I_j=|X_j|^2/N$ the periodogram there. Then $\int V_k(f-f')X(f')\dd f'=\frac1N\sum_jV_k(f-f_j)X_j$, and expanding the square in \eqref{eq:before} gives
\begin{multline}
\hat S_{\mathrm{mt}}(f)=\frac1N\sum_{j}H_K(f-f_j)\,\hat I_j\\
+\frac1{N^2}\sum_{j\neq l}B_K(f-f_j,f-f_l)\,X_jX_l^{*},
\label{eq:split}
\end{multline}
with $B_K(a,b)=\frac1K\sum_{k<K}V_k(a)V_k^{*}(b)$ and $H_K(a)=B_K(a,a)=\frac1K\sum_{k<K}|V_k(a)|^2$. The first term is the periodogram smoothed with $H_K$, the box with rounded shoulders of Fig.~\ref{fig:slepian}c, which is built from the $K$ tapers. The second is a cross term between different frequencies. For white noise the cross term has zero mean and is uncorrelated with the first term, so the variances of the two terms add. At a Fourier frequency the cross term carries the fraction
\begin{equation}
d^2=1-\frac{K}{N^2}\sum_jH_K(f_j)^2
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
of the variance of the multitaper estimate. The first term alone therefore has $\nu=2K/(1-d^2)$, and its correlation with the multitaper estimate is $(1-d^2)^{1/2}$. For $NW=4$ and $K=7$ these are $d^2=0.08$, $\nu=15.2$ and $0.96$. For $NW=2$ and $K=3$ they are $0.12$, $6.9$ and $0.94$. For a coloured spectrum the mean of the cross term is not zero. It is negative where the spectrum is low, because the cross term subtracts the power that the first term admits through the side lobes of the Fej\'er kernel. On the AR(4) process of Section~\ref{sec:simulations} the first term alone is about 20~dB too high in that region. The tapers act through the cross term (\texttt{demos/frequency\_domain\_multitaper.py}).

In terms of the matrix $Q$, an estimator has the form \eqref{eq:after} exactly when $Q$ is constant along each of its diagonals, that is, when $Q$ is a Toeplitz matrix. The matrix $A$ of Thomson's identity is of this kind, which is why the estimate with all $N$ tapers is a smoothed periodogram. The matrix of the $K$-taper estimate is not. The Toeplitz matrix closest to it is obtained by averaging each of its diagonals. It lies at a relative distance of $0.20$ for $NW=4$ and $K=7$, at any $N$, and at $0.27$ for the $NW=2$, $K=3$ of our EEG analysis.

For sine tapers \cite{riedel1995} the convolution in \eqref{eq:before} has only two terms, and the estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat S(f)=\frac{1}{2K(N+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl|X(f-\delta_k)-X(f+\delta_k)\bigr|^2,
\label{eq:sinefft}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_k=k/(2N+2)$ and the time origin one sample before the record. It is a multitaper estimate computed from the FFT of the untapered record, by differencing before squaring.

We asked whether a smoothed periodogram could nevertheless reproduce the estimate used in practice. For the expected value the answer is yes. The kernel of a quadratic estimator is the transform of the lag sums $q_\tau=\sum_tQ_{t,t+\tau}$ of its matrix (Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic}). For a tapered periodogram smoothed with the lag window $g_\tau$, the lag sums are $g_\tau r_w(\tau)$, where $r_w(\tau)=\sum_tw_tw_{t+\tau}$ is the autocorrelation of the taper. The lag window
\begin{equation}
g_\tau=\frac{q_\tau}{r_w(\tau)},\qquad q_\tau=\frac1K\sum_{k<K}\sum_tv_k(t)\,v_k(t+\tau),
\label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
therefore gives the smoothed periodogram the kernel of the $K$-taper estimate, exactly when $r_w(\tau)\neq0$ at every lag where $q_\tau\neq0$, as it is without a taper. Without a taper the estimator is the closest Toeplitz matrix described above. The symmetric Hann and cosine tapers vanish at their end samples, so the last two or three lags are not matched, and for $N=256$ the kernels then differ by less than $3\times10^{-4}$ of their peak. In the frequency domain, the smoothing kernel is the multitaper kernel $\frac1K\sum_k|V_k|^2$ deconvolved by the spectral window of the taper. The deconvolution is ill-conditioned at the lags where $r_w$ is small, so the lag window is applied in the lag domain. With an exact match the two estimators have the same bias for every spectrum.

They do not have the same variance. For real Gaussian white noise of unit variance, the variance of $x_f^{*}Qx_f$ is $\sum_{|\tau|<N}(1+\cos4\pi f\tau)\,e_\tau$ with $e_\tau=\sum_tQ_{t,t+\tau}^2$, and by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $e_\tau\ge q_\tau^2/(N-|\tau|)$, with equality when $Q$ is constant along the diagonal at lag $\tau$. For white noise, among all quadratic estimators with a given kernel, the smoothed periodogram without a taper therefore has the smallest variance at every frequency. For $N=256$ and $NW=4$, with the kernel of the $K$-taper estimate, it has $\nu=14.6$ for $K=7$ and $\nu=10.9$ for $K=4$. The multitaper estimates have $\nu=14$ and $\nu=8$. The result does not extend to coloured spectra. On the AR(4) process of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}, above 0.2 cycles per sample, the smoothed periodogram with the $K=7$ kernel has more than ten times the variance of the multitaper estimate. The matched kernel has negative lobes, and most of the eigen-weights of this estimator are negative (together about 3\% of the positive total). It has no protection against leakage.

The two are therefore different estimators, and their estimates of the same record differ. For $N=256$, $NW=4$ and $K=7$, the kernel-matched periodogram without a taper lies at a relative distance of $0.20$ from the multitaper matrix. The closest pair of taper and lag window, found by alternating least squares, lies at $0.15$, and its taper is nearly rectangular. On 1000 simulated records of the EEG-like spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}, the estimates of this pair differ from the multitaper estimates of the same records by $0.3$~dB (root mean square) and the kernel-matched estimates by $0.6$~dB. The error of each estimator is $2.0$~dB. On the AR(4) process, which spans 65~dB, the differences are $1.1$ and $4.0$~dB. The second value is computed over the two thirds of the kernel-matched estimates that are positive, because the other third are negative and have no value in decibels. Its lag window cancels the leakage of the untapered periodogram on average and not in each record. Hansson-Sandsten posed the same question for Welch's method \cite{hansson2012}, and our search over segment windows came no closer than $0.27$ (\texttt{demos/fit\_single\_window.py}, \texttt{demos/kernel\_matched.py}). A single window can therefore reproduce the multitaper estimate closely when the dynamic range is modest, and cannot when it is large. In that case the other routes must taper before they smooth or average.

The taper can be built from the Slepian tapers as well. The diagonal of the multitaper matrix is $\frac1K\sum_kv_k(t)^2$, so the taper $w_t=\bigl(\frac1K\sum_kv_k(t)^2\bigr)^{1/2}$ with the lag window \eqref{eq:matched} reproduces the diagonal and the kernel together. For $N=256$, $NW=4$ and $K=7$ this estimator lies at $0.16$ from the multitaper matrix. Its estimates differ from the multitaper estimates of the same records by $0.3$~dB on the EEG-like spectrum and by $0.8$~dB on the AR(4) process. None of them is negative, and its error equals that of the multitaper estimate on both processes (\texttt{demos/rounded\_kernel.py}).


\subsection{Simulations}
\label{sec:simulations}
We compared the estimators on a fourth-order autoregressive process, AR(4), that is a standard test of leakage \cite{percival1993}. Its PSD has two peaks $7/N$ wide at half power and $30/N$ apart for $N=1024$, on a background that spans 65~dB. We generated 300 realizations of $N=1024$ samples and estimated the PSD of each with a design half-bandwidth of $W=4/N$. The error of an estimate is the absolute difference in dB between the estimate and the true PSD. We report the median error over the whole band, the root-mean-square error over the peak region ($0.09$ to $0.16$ cycles per sample), and the median error where the spectrum is low ($f>0.3$). Kernel properties and degrees of freedom were computed exactly from the quadratic form of each estimator (Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic}), for $N=256$ and $W=4/N$ unless stated otherwise. The same comparison for $N=256$ and $N=128$ is in the repository. In the figures the smoothing route is recipe (b$'$) of Section~\ref{sec:recipe}, a 25\% cosine taper followed by a parabola whose half-power width is $2W$. The tables also give the Hann window followed by a box, which is the most familiar choice.

A comparison at one bandwidth leaves open whether a route would do better with other settings, so we also tuned each family. The families were multitaper estimates with Slepian or sine tapers \cite{riedel1995} and equal weights, for time--bandwidth products from 1 to 12 and every number of tapers; adaptively weighted Slepian estimates; one taper (Hann, a cosine taper of 10\%, 25\% or 50\%, or one Slepian taper) followed by a box or a parabola of 17 widths; two to five Slepian tapers followed by a box; and Welch's method with Hann segments of 12 lengths. For each family we shortlisted the five settings with the smallest error predicted from the exact mean and variance of the estimator (Appendix~\ref{app:quadratic}), scored them on 1000 simulated records, and kept the best. The adaptively weighted estimates are not quadratic and were scored on 500 records at every setting. The error is the root mean square, over records and frequencies, of the difference in dB between the estimate and the true PSD. The test cases were the AR(4) process with $N=256$ and $N=1024$, and an EEG-like spectrum sampled at 200~Hz with $N=400$ and $N=1024$. The EEG-like spectrum has a background that falls as $1/f^2$ above 3~Hz, an alpha peak at 10~Hz that is 1.4~Hz wide, and a noise floor. It spans 30~dB and was scored from 1 to 40~Hz. The tuning uses the true spectrum, so it shows what each route can achieve and is not a procedure for choosing settings.

\subsection{EEG Data and Settings}
\label{sec:eegmethods}
Figs.~\ref{fig:eeg} and \ref{fig:eeg2} apply the three practical estimators to two ten-minute scalp EEG clips that contain focal seizures. Clinicians read spectrograms of recordings like these. The clips are de-identified recordings from a seizure-annotation set (ELROND) drawn from the Brain Data Science Platform. They were sampled at 200~Hz on 20 channels, and one of the authors (M.B.W.) marked the seizure onsets and offsets. Retrospective analysis of the EEG data was conducted under Institutional Review Board protocols at Stanford University (\#83833), Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (\#2016P000058) and Massachusetts General Hospital (\#2013P001024), with a waiver of consent. For each clip we show the channel with the largest ictal rise in power between 2 and 20~Hz. The features to preserve are the harmonics of the ictal rhythm. At the ictal instant of Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg} the fundamental is at $7.7$~Hz and the harmonics are $7.4$~Hz apart. On the unsmoothed periodogram the peaks are $0.5$ to $0.7$~Hz wide. This is the resolution of a 2-second window, $0.5$~Hz, and an upper bound on the width of the rhythm itself. In the second clip the harmonic spacing falls to about 4~Hz as the seizure slows. Following the rule of Section~\ref{sec:howwide}, the bandwidth should be well below the harmonic spacing and otherwise as wide as possible. We set $2W=2$~Hz in 2-second windows. The multitaper estimate uses $NW=2$, $K=3$ and equal weights, the smoothed estimate uses recipe (b$'$), a 25\% cosine taper with a parabola whose half-power width is 2~Hz, and Welch's method uses 0.7-second Hann segments. Table~\ref{tab:bandpower} also gives the Hann taper with a box of half-width 1~Hz.

Fig.~\ref{fig:sleep} uses 40~s of stage N2 sleep from a de-identified overnight polysomnogram from the same platform, sampled at 200~Hz, channel C4 referred to the left mastoid. The stage was scored by a technologist and agreed with automated staging. We marked spindles where the envelope of the signal between 11 and 16~Hz exceeded twice its median for 0.5 to 3~s. The spectrograms use windows of 1, 2 and 4~s stepped by 0.1~s, with design bandwidths $2W$ of 2, 2 and 1~Hz.

\paragraph{Leakage survives smoothing and averaging} The box-smoothed untapered periodogram has the intended resolution and the most degrees of freedom. Where the spectrum is low it errs by 13.7~dB (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}), because the leakage in the Fej\'er tails is averaged and remains in the estimate, as \eqref{eq:corollary} predicts. A tapered periodogram smoothed to the same width errs by 1.0~dB in that region with the cosine taper and by 1.4~dB with the Hann window. The taper controls the skirts of the kernel, and the box or segment length controls the width of its main lobe. Neither can substitute for the other. Prewhitening is the other classical remedy for a large dynamic range \cite{blackman1958,percival1993}, and it can be combined with any of the three routes.

\paragraph{A heavy taper costs degrees of freedom} At the spectral peaks leakage is negligible and the errors follow $\nu$. The multitaper estimate, with $\nu=14.0$, errs by 1.8~dB. The Hann-tapered, box-smoothed periodogram has $\nu=8.9$, a third fewer, because its ladder slopes, and it errs by 2.3~dB. The corresponding relative standard errors $\sqrt{2/\nu}$ are 38\% and 47\%. The cost can be paid in resolution instead. If the box is widened until the Hann route reaches $\nu=14$, its kernel is $13/N$ wide, compared with $7.4/N$ for Thomson's. The deficit decreases as $N$ grows, because the broadening caused by a single taper scales as $1/N$ and $W$ does not \cite{riedel1994}.

\paragraph{The cost belongs to the Hann window} The Hann window gives most of the record little weight, and a lighter taper recovers the loss. The 25\% cosine taper followed by a parabola of the same half-power width has $\nu=16.7$. With the box in place of the parabola it has $\nu=14.8$, which is still more than the multitaper estimate. The parabola adds the rest because its base is wider than that of a box of the same half-power width. It errs by 1.7~dB at the peaks and by 1.0~dB where the spectrum is low, which is less than any multitaper estimate in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}. Its skirts are higher than those of the Hann route and lower than those of the multitaper estimate: beyond $3W$ the three kernels are below $-38$, $-66$ and $-31$~dB. The price is the shape of the kernel. A parabola has no flat top and its base is 1.4 times its half-power width, so two equal tones are separated at $10.0/N$, compared with $9.0/N$ for the Hann-tapered box.

\paragraph{The last Slepian taper leaks} For $NW=4$ the seventh taper has $\lambda_6=0.94$, so 6\% of its energy lies outside the band. This taper sets the skirt of the multitaper kernel at $-22$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:everyday}f), and the kernel is still at $-31$~dB beyond $3W$, where that of the smoothed periodogram is below $-38$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:everyday}g). With equal weights and $K=2NW-1$, the multitaper estimate errs by 6.2~dB where the spectrum is low, compared with 1.0~dB for the smoothed periodogram. On records of 128 samples the errors are 15~dB and 1.2~dB. Dropping the last taper reduces the multitaper error to 2.1~dB. Thomson's adaptive weights \cite{thomson1982,thomson1991} reduce it to 1.7~dB by down-weighting the leaky tapers at frequencies where the spectrum is far below its maximum. At those frequencies the adaptively weighted estimate has $\nu=6.5$ instead of 14 (Fig.~S2). Recent analysis recommends $K=2NW-O(\log NW)$ for the same reason \cite{karnik2022}. Over the whole band the adaptively weighted estimate and the Hann-tapered, smoothed periodogram have the same error, 1.4~dB, and the cosine-tapered parabola errs by 1.0~dB. The Hann-tapered, smoothed periodogram therefore matches the adaptive estimator where the spectrum is low, and has a third fewer degrees of freedom where it is high.

\paragraph{Welch's method and hybrid estimators} Welch's method with half-overlapping Hann segments, matched in half-power width to the other estimators, has the most degrees of freedom in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} ($\nu=17.6$) and, with the cosine-tapered parabola, the smallest errors. Its segments are nearly orthogonal, so its ladder is nearly flat. Its disadvantage is the shape of its kernel. The spectral window of a short Hann segment has no flat top, and the base of its main lobe, $4/L$, is almost three times its half-power width. As a result it separates two equal tones only when they are $10.8/N$ apart, compared with $9.0/N$ for the Hann-tapered box of the same half-power width. Bronez found that multitaper estimation outperforms Welch's method when both resolution and leakage are matched \cite{bronez1992}. The two findings are consistent, because giving the Welch kernel sharp edges requires longer segments, and $\nu$ then falls below Thomson's. The routes can also be combined. Riedel, Sidorenko and Thomson found that a few tapers followed by smoothing performed best on plasma data \cite{riedel1994}, and we find the same. Two well-concentrated Slepian tapers ($NW=2$) followed by the box give $\nu=13.3$, a side lobe at $-34$~dB, and errors of 1.3, 1.9 and 1.6~dB.

\subsection{Each Route at Its Best Setting}
\label{sec:tuned}
Table~\ref{tab:tuned} gives the smallest error that each family reached when its parameters were tuned. In each of the four cases the tapered families lie within 20\% of one another, and a single taper followed by smoothing is never behind the multitaper estimate. Multitaper estimates with Slepian tapers and equal weights are 3 to 10\% behind the single taper followed by smoothing, and adaptively weighted ones are 4 to 19\% behind. The adaptive weights protect a default setting against leakage. A setting that has been tuned does not need them.

The best single taper was a cosine taper of 10\% or 25\% in every case. The Hann window was 17 to 24\% behind it, and the parabola was 2 to 4\% ahead of the box (\texttt{demos/taper\_and\_kernel.py}). The 25\% cosine taper was within 3\% of the best taper in every case, including records of 128 samples of the AR(4) process, for which the 10\% taper leaks. Without any taper, the smoothed periodogram is the most accurate estimator on the EEG-like spectrum and the least accurate, by 8 to 11~dB, on the AR(4) process.

The kernel of \eqref{eq:matched} was tuned in the same way, over the taper and over the multitaper estimate to which it is matched. Matched to the best multitaper estimate, it reproduces the error of that estimate: 1.96 and 1.40~dB on the EEG-like spectrum, against 1.97 and 1.40~dB. Its own best settings are matched to multitaper estimates with a large time--bandwidth product and few tapers, whose kernels are bell-shaped, and they come within 6\% of the parabola. On the AR(4) process it requires a taper. Without one, more than a tenth of its estimates are negative at every setting. A kernel matched to the multitaper estimate is therefore the right choice for reproducing that estimate, and not for improving on it. The parabola \eqref{eq:parabola} is at least as accurate, has one parameter, and cannot give a negative estimate.

The lower rows of the table give the estimators at the bandwidth used in the figures. At that bandwidth recipe (b$'$) has a smaller error than the adaptively weighted multitaper estimate in all four cases.

The settings above use one width at all frequencies. The best width \eqref{eq:bopt} varies with the curvature of the spectrum, so we also chose the width separately at each frequency, on one set of records, and scored the choice on a second set. With a 25\% cosine taper and a parabola the error falls from 2.42, 1.41, 1.94 and 1.41~dB in the four cases to 1.69, 1.01, 1.39 and 1.00~dB, a gain of 28 to 30\%. The multitaper estimate with equal weights falls to 1.77, 1.15, 1.42 and 1.08~dB, and with adaptive weights to 1.87, 1.26, 1.47 and 1.17~dB. The gain from choosing the width well is several times the difference between the routes. The choice uses the true spectrum, so these values are bounds and not the results of a procedure. A width that varies with frequency is simple to apply to a periodogram. A multitaper estimate requires a different set of tapers for every width (\texttt{demos/local\_width.py}).

\subsection{EEG With Seizures}
\label{sec:eeg}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg} the ictal rhythm begins near 7.5~Hz with harmonics up to 25~Hz, slows over about a minute, and ends in post-ictal suppression. This descending pattern is often called a chirp and is typical of an evolving seizure. Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg2} contains two such seizures, each with a stack of harmonics that descends from 20~Hz to 3~Hz. These descriptions are based on reading the spectrograms. The unsmoothed periodogram shows the chirps but is very noisy. The multitaper and smoothed-periodogram spectrograms cannot be told apart by inspection, and the spectra at a pre-ictal and an ictal instant (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg}d,\,f) show all three practical estimators resolving the same harmonics. Over all $599$ windows the two spectrograms differ by a median of $0.9$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg}h) and $0.7$~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg2}). With the Hann taper and the box the medians are $1.5$ and $1.2$~dB. These differences are smaller than the noise of the estimators, which is $2.5$ and $2.1$~dB for $\nu=6$ and $\nu=8.7$, because the two estimates share most of their noise.

Band powers agree more closely than individual spectral values, because integration over a band averages the noise that distinguishes the estimators (Table~\ref{tab:bandpower}). In Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg} the ictal rise in theta, alpha and beta power is 17 to 18~dB by every estimator, and the estimators agree to within $0.7$~dB. The disagreement with the multitaper estimate in individual windows is about $1$~dB for the Hann route, $0.5$~dB for Welch's method, and $0.1$ to $0.3$~dB for recipe (b$'$). Conclusions drawn from band powers in these recordings would not depend on the route.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Recipes for the Three Routes}
\label{sec:recipe}
The three routes are close enough in accuracy that the choice among them can be made on practical grounds. In the boxes below, $N$ is the number of samples and $2W$ is the desired resolution, expressed as a full width in cycles per sample (divide by the sampling interval to obtain hertz). We recommend setting $2W$ to about the width of the narrowest feature that must be preserved (Section~\ref{sec:howwide}), and the window length to the duration of the briefest event that must be resolved in time. The degrees of freedom then follow from \eqref{eq:design}.

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Exact versions.} These give the same estimate, to machine precision for (a) and (b) and to a fraction of a decibel for (c). The windows in (b) and (c) follow from requiring equality with (a) (identity \eqref{eq:identity}, Appendix~\ref{app:welch}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] \emph{Multitaper.} Compute all $N$ Slepian tapers for $(N,W)$ and their concentrations $\lambda_k$. Average the eigenspectra with weights $\lambda_k/2NW$.
\item[(b)] \emph{Smooth.} Compute the periodogram of the untapered record, zero-padded to at least $2N$ points. Convolve it along frequency with a box of half-width $W$.
\item[(c)] \emph{Average.} Use a sinc window $w_t=2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2Wt)$ many times longer than the record. Place it at every integer offset, including offsets at which it extends beyond the zero-padded ends. Sum the squared transforms and divide by $2NW$.
\end{enumerate}
The estimate that these share contains the leakage of the untapered periodogram.
\end{framed}

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Versions for practical use.} These remove the leakage in three different ways, so they are close but not identical.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a$'$)] \emph{Multitaper.} Keep the $K=2NW-1$ tapers with $\lambda_k\approx1$ and weight them adaptively, or keep $K=2NW-2$ with equal weights. Expect $\nu\approx2K$ where the spectrum is high and about half that where it is far below its maximum.
\item[(b$'$)] \emph{Smooth.} Taper the first and last eighth of the record with half a cosine (a 25\% cosine, or Tukey, taper) and scale the taper to unit energy. Compute the squared FFT, zero-padded to at least $2N$ points, or more for a finer display grid. Convolve with a parabola of half-width $\sqrt2\,W$, whose half-power width is $2W$. When each kernel is used at its best width, the parabola has the smallest asymptotic mean square error of any non-negative kernel (Section~\ref{sec:shape}). At the fixed half-power width used here it has 20\% more bias and 15\% less variance than the box. Expect $\nu\approx2\cdot2NW$, a kernel without a flat top whose base is 1.4 times its half-power width, and skirts whose level beyond $3W$ depends on the time--bandwidth product. For any $N$ they are below $-25$, $-31$ and $-38$~dB for $2NW=4$, 6 and 8. A box of half-width $W$ has the same half-power width and a flat top, and was 2 to 4\% less accurate in Table~\ref{tab:tuned}. If the dynamic range is much larger than 65~dB, use a Hann window. Its skirts are at least 25~dB lower, and it halves $\nu$.
\item[(c$'$)] \emph{Average.} Use Hann-tapered segments of length $L\approx1.4/(2W)$ that overlap by half, and average their periodograms. Expect $\nu$ of about twice the number of segments, a kernel with no flat top whose main lobe has a base of $4/L$, and leakage set by the Hann window of length $L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{framed}

We suggest route (a$'$) when the dynamic range is unknown and may be large, when Thomson's F-test for spectral lines is needed, or when coherence and cross-spectra are required, because the statistical properties of multitaper cross-spectral estimates are well established \cite{walden2000}. Route (b$'$) is preferable when the bandwidth should vary with frequency, which is difficult to arrange with tapers or segments, and for time--frequency displays, where smoothing along both axes is natural. It is also the easiest of the three to interpret. Route (c$'$) suits long records and pipelines that already compute it. At matched half-power width it has the most degrees of freedom and the poorest two-tone resolution. For any route, $\nu$ determines the error bar. The 95\% confidence interval of Section~\ref{sec:periodogram} extends from $-3.3$ to $+5.3$~dB around the estimate for $\nu=8.9$, from $-2.7$ to $+4.0$~dB for $\nu=14$, and from $-2.5$ to $+3.6$~dB for $\nu=16.7$.


\subsection{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:prior}
\paragraph{Established results} The results assembled here are established, but they are scattered across sources that most users of spectral estimation do not read. Identity \eqref{eq:identity} is Thomson's equation (8.3) \cite{thomson1982}, which takes up half a page of a forty-page paper. The fact that any quadratic estimator is a weighted multitaper estimator, with the eigenvectors of its matrix as tapers, appears in the textbook of Percival and Walden \cite{percival1993}. Riedel and Sidorenko developed it for kernel smoothers \cite{riedel1995} and Walden for multivariate estimators \cite{walden2000}. The degrees-of-freedom formula \eqref{eq:ladder} is Satterthwaite's approximation \cite{satterthwaite1946} applied to this decomposition. Welch showed that averaging the periodograms of a sliding window smooths the periodogram \cite{welch1967}, and Nuttall and Carter gave the general form with combined time and lag weighting \cite{nuttall1982}. Hansson-Sandsten approximated the matrix of the Thomson estimator by that of a Welch estimator, in the mean-square sense, for real-time use \cite{hansson2012}. Thomson and Chave remarked that section averaging and multiple-window estimates are mathematically similar \cite{thomson1991}. Abreu and Romero proved that the $K$-taper kernel converges to a box and gave the rate \cite{abreu2017}. Karnik, Romberg and Davenport used \eqref{eq:identity} in matrix form in a fast multitaper algorithm \cite{karnik2022}. Riedel, Sidorenko and Thomson compared multitaper estimates with smoothed tapered periodograms \cite{riedel1994}, and Bronez compared them with Welch's method \cite{bronez1992}. Harris catalogued the properties of windows \cite{harris1978}. Most of this literature is written in terms of eigenvalue problems, error rates and matrix algorithms. The tutorials, software and textbooks from which neuroscientists learn spectral estimation \cite{mitra1999,bokil2010,babadi2014,prerau2017,kay1988} do not relate the three methods to one another.

\paragraph{The view that smoothing is obsolete} Thomson and Chave wrote that periodograms and smoothed periodograms are ``hopelessly obsolete'' \cite{thomson1991}, and many users regard multitaper estimation as a different and better method. The statement is correct for a periodogram without a taper. By \eqref{eq:identity} that estimate is a multitaper estimate with all $N$ tapers, and Thomson observed that the poorly concentrated ones bias it \cite{thomson1982}. In Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} it errs by 13.7~dB where the spectrum is low. The statement does not hold for a tapered periodogram. The Hann-tapered, smoothed periodogram errs by 1.4~dB in the same region, and the adaptively weighted multitaper estimate by 1.7~dB. With a Hann taper the smoothed periodogram gives up efficiency instead. At the same design bandwidth it has $\nu=8.9$ and the multitaper estimate has 14. The loss belongs to the Hann window and not to smoothing. With a 25\% cosine taper the smoothed periodogram has $\nu=14.8$ with the box and $16.7$ with the parabola, and when every route is tuned it is at least as accurate as the multitaper estimate in each of our test cases (Table~\ref{tab:tuned}). Riedel, Sidorenko and Thomson likewise found a smoothed estimate with a few tapers to be the most accurate of those they compared \cite{riedel1994}. Multitaper estimation is one member of a family of estimators of similar accuracy. Its particular strengths are a bound on the leakage of each taper that holds for any spectrum, the F-test for spectral lines, and an established theory for cross-spectra.

\paragraph{Contributions of this paper} We combine these results in a single description, the bank of tapers, and work out its practical consequences. The sliding-window form of the identity relates the averaging route exactly to the other two, and identifies the Slepian sequences as the principal components of a sliding sinc window. The eigen-weight ladder explains a standard degrees-of-freedom formula in terms that apply to all three routes, since the variance cost of a single taper corresponds to the slope of its ladder. Tables~\ref{tab:ensemble} and \ref{tab:dof} list the cost of each route and the conditions under which each performs best, including the adaptively weighted estimate. The bandwidth rule and the recipes allow the parameters to be chosen from the features of the data. Bruns showed in a similar way that Fourier, Hilbert and wavelet analyses of neural signals are formally equivalent \cite{bruns2004}. The bank-of-tapers description covers those methods as well. A Morlet scalogram, for example, is a single-taper periodogram whose Gaussian taper shortens as frequency increases.


\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The identity is exact only for the eigenvalue-weighted estimate with all $N$ tapers, and its sliding-window form only in the limit of a long window. The versions used in practice are close but not equal. A smoothing kernel matched to the multitaper kernel removes the difference in expected value and not the difference in individual estimates.

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} uses a single process, a fourth-order autoregression with two peaks and a range of 65~dB. The estimators are compared at matched design bandwidth and not at matched $\nu$. The median error over the whole band is dominated by the region of low power, where leakage is largest. For this reason the table also reports the error at the peaks, where the ranking of the estimators is reversed. Table~\ref{tab:tuned} adds a second spectrum and tunes every route, but the tuning uses the true spectrum, which is not available in practice, and it scores one measure of error over the whole band. Other spectra, spectral lines and very short records will rank the routes differently. The adaptive weights are those of Thomson's original iteration. We did not use the jackknife error estimates \cite{thomson1991} or the quadratic-inverse estimates \cite{thomson1990} that were developed later.

The kernels, degrees of freedom and leakage values describe the estimators for a stationary Gaussian process and were computed on a finite FFT grid. The spectrograms assume stationarity within each 2-second window, an assumption that fails at the onset of a seizure. The EEG evidence consists of two clips with one channel each, analyzed with equal-weight multitaper estimates. We did not consider coherence or cross-spectra, inference beyond the $\chi^2_\nu$ interval, or data-driven bandwidth selection.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Averaging periodograms, smoothing a periodogram and multitaper estimation are the same estimator, a bank of tapers with weights that can be built from one window in three ways. When all Slepian tapers are used with their eigenvalues as weights, the multitaper estimate equals a smoothed periodogram exactly, and in the limit of a long window it equals an average of sliding-window periodograms. A multitaper estimate smooths the Fourier transform before squaring it, and a smoothed periodogram smooths after. The estimator used in practice is this estimate with its leaky components removed. This description expresses the multitaper parameters in terms that users of the other two methods already know. The taper controls the skirts of the kernel, and the box width or segment length controls the width of its main lobe. The bandwidth is chosen by balancing blur against noise. A lightly tapered and smoothed periodogram controls leakage as well as an adaptively weighted multitaper estimate, and is as accurate when both are tuned. A heavy taper such as the Hann window halves the degrees of freedom. Among non-negative smoothing kernels, each at its best width, the parabola has the smallest asymptotic mean square error, and for white noise no quadratic estimator with a given kernel has a smaller variance than the untapered smoothed periodogram. Without a taper, a smoothed or averaged periodogram is biased by leakage wherever the spectrum is low. For most EEG applications any of the three routes is adequate, and the choice can be made for convenience.

\section{Proof of the Identity}
\label{app:proof}
\label{app:proof}
Let $A$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with entries $A_{tt'}=\sin(2\pi W(t-t'))/(\pi(t-t'))$ and $A_{tt}=2W$. The Slepian sequences are its eigenvectors, $Av_k=\lambda_kv_k$, $\|v_k\|=1$ \cite{slepian1978}, so $A=\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ and $\sum_k\lambda_k=\operatorname{tr}A=2NW$. With $x_f$ the modulated data, $\hat S_k(f)=|v_k^{\!\top}x_f|^2$, and
\begin{align}
\sum_{k}\lambda_k\hat S_k(f)&=x_f^{*}\Bigl(\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}\Bigr)x_f\notag\\
&=\sum_{t,t'}A_{tt'}\,x_tx_{t'}\,e^{-i2\pi f(t-t')}\notag\\
&=\sum_{\tau=-(N-1)}^{N-1}h_\tau\,\hat s_\tau\,e^{-i2\pi f\tau},
\end{align}
with $h_\tau=\sin(2\pi W\tau)/(\pi\tau)$ and $\hat s_\tau=\sum_tx_tx_{t+\tau}$, collecting terms by lag. Now $\hat s_\tau$ is the inverse transform of $N\hat I(f)$ and $h_\tau$ the inverse transform of the indicator of $[-W,W]$; a product of lag sequences transforms to a convolution in frequency, so the sum equals $\int_{f-W}^{f+W}N\hat I(f')\dd f'$. Divide by $2NW$. $\square$


\section{Kernels, Banks and Variance of Quadratic Estimators}
\label{app:quadratic}
\label{app:quadratic}
Every estimator in this paper is $\hat S(f)=x_f^{*}Qx_f$ with $Q$ real symmetric positive semidefinite and independent of $f$ \cite{percival1993,riedel1995}: $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ for a multitaper estimate; $Q_{tt'}=w_tg_{t-t'}w_{t'}$ for a periodogram tapered with $w$ and smoothed with the lag window $g$ (the inverse transform of the kernel); $Q=\sum_su_su_s^{\!\top}/\sum_s\|u_s\|^2$ for averaged periodograms of a window placed at offsets $s$.

\emph{Bank.} $Q=\sum_kc_kv_kv_k^{\!\top}$ with orthonormal $v_k$ and $c_k\ge0$, so every $Q$ is a multitaper estimate \eqref{eq:bank}.

\emph{Kernel.} $\E[\hat S(f)]=\int H(f-f')S(f')\dd f'$ with $H$ the transform of the lag sums $q_\tau=\sum_tQ_{t,t+\tau}$; equivalently $H=\sum_kc_k|V_k|^2$. For a tapered, smoothed periodogram $H=|\mathcal{W}|^2*G$; for averaged periodograms $H=|\mathcal{W}_L|^2$ whatever the overlap; for $Q=A$, $H=B_W*F$, which is why two rows of Table~\ref{tab:dof} coincide.

\emph{Degrees of freedom.} For unit white Gaussian noise, $\hat S(f)$ has mean $\operatorname{tr}Q_f$ and variance $2\operatorname{tr}Q_f^2$, with $Q_f$ the real part of $Q$ conjugated by the modulation, so $\nu=(\operatorname{tr}Q_f)^2/\operatorname{tr}Q_f^2$, the chi-square approximation of Satterthwaite \cite{satterthwaite1946}. At an interior frequency each modulated taper is orthogonal to its conjugate, each term of the bank is an independent $\chi^2_2$, and $\nu=2(\sum c_k)^2/\sum c_k^2$, equation~\eqref{eq:ladder}. The two formulas agree to within 3\% for the three estimators tested (multitaper, Hann-then-box and Welch); Table~\ref{tab:dof} uses the first, and Monte Carlo on white noise (3000 draws) agrees with it to within 15\% for the same three (repository tests).

\emph{Resolution and leakage.} Resolution is the half-power full width of $H$; leakage is the fraction of $H$ outside a stated band; the peak side lobe is the maximum of $H$ beyond $1.5\times$ the half-power half-width. A practitioner's convolution of a zero-padded periodogram with a kernel on the same grid is exactly the lag-window estimate above once the padding reaches $2N$ points, because the periodogram's lag sequence vanishes beyond $|\tau|=N-1$.


\section{Averaging Over a Sliding Window Is Smoothing}
\label{app:welch}
\label{app:welch}

\subsection{Continuous Time: the Limit of All Overlaps}
Let $x(t)$ be the record, zero outside $[0,T]$, with transform $X(\omega)$, and $w$ a real symmetric window with transform $W$. Windowing at shift $\tau$ gives $s_\tau(t)=x(t)w(t-\tau)$, with transform
\begin{equation*}
S_\tau(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int X(\omega')W(\omega-\omega')e^{-i(\omega-\omega')\tau}\dd\omega'.
\end{equation*} Welch's method with shifts $n\Delta$ averages $|S_{n\Delta}(\omega)|^2$ over $n$. Let the overlap grow without bound, $\Delta\to0$: the average becomes a Riemann sum for an integral over the shift, which can be evaluated in closed form,
\begin{align}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}&|S_\tau(\omega)|^2\dd\tau\notag\\
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint X(\omega')X^{*}(\omega'')\,W(\omega-\omega')W^{*}(\omega-\omega'')\notag\\
&\qquad\times\Bigl[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\,i(\omega'-\omega'')\tau}\dd\tau\Bigr]\dd\omega'\dd\omega''\notag\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int|X(\omega')|^2\,|W(\omega-\omega')|^2\dd\omega'\notag\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl(|W|^2*|X|^2\bigr)(\omega),
\label{eq:continuous}
\end{align}
because the bracket is $2\pi\delta(\omega'-\omega'')$. The sum of the periodograms of all shifted windows is the raw periodogram smoothed with the window's own spectral window; dividing by $T\!\int\!w^2$, the trace of the quadratic form, makes the estimate unbiased for white noise. The shift integral must run over every $\tau$ at which the window touches the record, including shifts at which it extends beyond the ends. These shifts are needed to produce the delta function, and restricting $\tau$ to $[0,T]$ leaves an edge term that vanishes only when $T$ is much longer than the window. For a Gaussian window of standard deviation $\sigma_t$ the kernel is Gaussian with standard deviation $1/(2\sqrt2\,\pi\sigma_t)$ cycles per unit time, so sliding a Gaussian window is equivalent to smoothing with a Gaussian. This calculation, with a Gaussian window, was the content of an unpublished 2001 note by one of us, written before the connection to multitaper estimation was known; the result is Welch's \cite{welch1967} and is contained in Nuttall and Carter's framework \cite{nuttall1982}.

\subsection{Discrete Time and a Finite Record}
In discrete time the shift is an integer, and the sum over all shifts gives the result exactly, with no limit required. Let $w$ have length $L$ and $u_s$ be the length-$N$ vector holding $w$ at offset $s$, with the parts of $w$ outside the record discarded. Averaging over offsets gives $Q_{tt'}=\sum_sw[t-s]w[t'-s]$. If the offsets run over every integer for which the window touches the record, $s=-(L-1),\dots,N-1$, the sum equals the autocorrelation $r_w(t-t')$ for every pair inside the record, so $Q$ is Toeplitz and the estimator is the lag-window estimate of the untapered periodogram with lag window $r_w$ and kernel $|\mathcal{W}(f)|^2$. This is exact for a finite record; with a larger step or without overhang it holds approximately in the interior. The equivalence is for the \emph{untapered} periodogram: a tapered periodogram that is then smoothed has $Q_{tt'}=w_tg_{t-t'}w_{t'}$, which is not Toeplitz and is not the average of any single sliding window, though it is a time-and-lag weighting in the sense of \cite{nuttall1982}. For the sinc window, $r_w(\tau)\to2W\,\mathrm{sinc}(2W\tau)$ as $L\to\infty$, the lag sequence of $A$; the relative error in $Q$ falls as $1/L$ because the truncated sinc's autocorrelation differs from the sinc by a term of that order. Hence \eqref{eq:sliding}, and the Slepian sequences are the principal components of the shifted sinc windows.


\section{The Kernel of Least Mean Square Error}
\label{app:variational}
Let the spectrum be smooth near $f$ with relative curvature $c=|S''(f)/S(f)|$, and let $G$ be a non-negative kernel of unit area that is much wider than $1/N$. To leading order the relative mean square error of the smoothed periodogram is
\begin{equation}
J[G]=\frac{c^2}{4}\Bigl(\int u^2G\dd u\Bigr)^2+\frac{c_w}{N}\int G^2\dd u .
\label{eq:functional}
\end{equation}
The first term is the squared bias and the second is the variance. We minimize $J$ subject to $\int G\dd u=1$ and $G\ge0$. With a multiplier $\lambda$ for the first constraint, the first variation vanishes wherever $G>0$ if
\begin{equation}
\frac{c^2}{2}\,\mu_2\,u^2+\frac{2c_w}{N}\,G(u)-\lambda=0,\qquad\mu_2=\int u^2G\dd u .
\end{equation}
The minimizer is therefore a parabola, $G(u)=A(b^2-u^2)$ for $|u|\le b$ and zero elsewhere, with $A=Nc^2\mu_2/(4c_w)$. Unit area requires $A=3/(4b^3)$, and the parabola has $\mu_2=b^2/5$. Together these give $b^5=15c_w/(Nc^2)$, which is \eqref{eq:bopt}. Both terms of $J$ are convex in $G$, so the stationary point is the minimum. One calculation gives the shape and the width. The shape does not depend on the spectrum, and the width depends on it only through the curvature $c$.
