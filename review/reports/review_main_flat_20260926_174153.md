# VSNC

## Vision — ⚠️
The big idea is stated, unmissably and early:

> "This paper shows that the three methods are one method (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract})."

That is a textbook vision sentence. Two gaps.

**(a) The empowerment promise arrives on page 1 of the abstract's *second* paragraph and is phrased passively:**

> "Practitioners may therefore use whichever route their pipeline already computes, including frequency-dependent bandwidths and two-axis smoothing of spectrograms, at a known cost in variance."

That is the payoff for the reader, and it is the 11th sentence. Move it up and make it second-person-explicit. Suggested insertion after the "we prove" sentence:

> *"A reader who finishes this paper can do three things: choose any of the three routes on grounds of convenience alone, read the variance cost of that choice off a single number, and set the bandwidth from the narrowest spectral feature that matters."*

**(b) "What concrete advances does this enable?" is under-claimed.** The paper enables at least two things it never says out loud: (i) results computed with different pipelines become *comparable* rather than merely similar, and (ii) frequency-dependent bandwidths and two-axis spectrogram smoothing — which have *no multitaper counterpart* — become licensed rather than suspect. The Limitations section actually admits this is a *feature*:

> "Frequency-dependent bandwidths and smoothing along time as well as frequency are natural in the smoothing route and have no exact multitaper counterpart, which is a reason to prefer that route rather than a limitation of the analysis"

Promote that sentence out of Limitations and into the Vision. It is a capability claim buried in a caveats list.

## Steps — ⚠️
The steps *exist* and are excellent — they are just hidden mid-paragraph in the introduction:

> "The method throughout is one: write each estimator as a quadratic form, diagonalize it, and compare through its kernel and its ladder."

This is the whole executable recipe of the paper in eighteen words, and it appears in the *middle* of the fifth sentence of a contributions paragraph. The abstract has no counterpart at all — it jumps from "We prove that the three methods are one estimator" straight to results. Add to the abstract:

> *"The method is the same in each case: write the estimator as a quadratic form, diagonalize it, and compare estimators through two objects — the expected-value kernel and the ladder of eigen-weights."*

The user-facing steps (the two `framed` recipe boxes in §6) are very good and are the strongest Steps material in the paper. Consider forward-referencing them from the abstract by name ("an exact trio and an everyday trio").

## News — ✅ (with a density warning)
Maximum specificity throughout; this is exemplary:

> "on a spectrum spanning 65~dB the box-smoothed untapered periodogram floors 20~dB high (median error 11.6~dB) while the same box on a Hann-tapered periodogram tracks it to 1.3~dB"
> "$\nu=8.9$ against $14.0$ at $N=256$, $NW=4$"
> "agree to a median of 1.5~dB over the spectrogram and to within 0.7~dB in band power"

The failure mode here is the *opposite* of the usual one: the abstract carries roughly fifteen numbers and three parenthetical qualifications, and no single number survives a cold read. Winston's test is whether the reader can repeat one fact at dinner. Pick three headline numbers — I would choose **1.3 dB vs 4.6 dB** (the surprise), **8.9 vs 14.0** (the price), and **1.5 dB on EEG** (the validation) — and demote the rest to the introduction, which already repeats them all anyway.

One precision note against the title. The title asserts "Are One Estimator," and the Limitations section correctly walks it back:

> "The identity is exact for the eigenvalue-weighted estimate with all $N$ tapers and holds for the everyday estimator only up to the leaky remainder"

The abstract handles this honestly ("One correspondence is exact"), but a skimmer who reads only the title will over-generalize. Consider a subtitle-level hedge or, better, keep the title and add one clause to the abstract's opening claim: *"We prove that the three are instances of one estimator, and that one pair of them coincides exactly, to machine precision, on every record."*

## Contributions — ✅
Best-in-class. The verbs are sanctioned, italicized, and enumerated:

> "We make five contributions. We \emph{prove} that... We \emph{derive}... and \emph{identify}... We \emph{identify} the eigen-weight ladder... We \emph{compute} exact kernels... And we \emph{present} a recipe... \emph{explain} how to choose its bandwidth... and \emph{test} it on scalp EEG"

Abstract closes with "We implement and test all three routes and both identities in a Python package that reproduces every figure." — correct verbs, verifiable claim. No change needed.

# THE 5 S's

## Slogan — ⚠️
The paper has **six** candidate slogans and therefore none:

1. "the three methods are one estimator" (title, abstract, §5, conclusion)
2. "a bank of tapers with weights" (abstract, §5.1, conclusion)
3. "Three routes, one estimate" (Fig. 9 caption, §6 title)
4. "the one picture that explains it" (§2 title)
5. "the eigen-weight ladder" (abstract, §5.1)
6. "blur against noise" (§3.4, conclusion)

**Recommendation:** promote exactly one to slogan status and demote the rest to ordinary prose. My vote is **"a bank of tapers with weights"** — it is mechanistic, it is the actual content of the proof, and it is the sentence that does the explanatory work. Then:
- Retire "the one picture" (rename §2 to *"The periodogram, and the kernel picture"*).
- Use "three routes, one estimate" only as the figure-1 title (it already is).
- Keep "eigen-weight ladder" as a *coined term*, not a slogan — that is the right role for it, and it is well-served by being named once and used consistently.

Also: the paper invents a genuinely memorable mnemonic and then buries it in §5.4:

> "Of the three, one is a warning, one is a price, and one is a surprise; if a reader remembers one, it should be the surprise."

**Warning / price / surprise** is a better organizing device than anything in the abstract. Use it three times: as the structure of the abstract's second paragraph, as the three `\paragraph` headings in §5.4 (they currently read "Smoothing and averaging cannot undo leakage" / "A single taper costs variance" / "The last Slepian taper leaks" — retitle as *"The warning: smoothing cannot undo leakage"*, etc.), and in the conclusion.

## Symbol — ⚠️
There are strong figures but no *recurring* one. Fig. 9 (graphical abstract, three overlaid PSD curves) and Fig. 6 (three taper banks + three ladders) are competing for the role, and they look nothing alike, so neither becomes the paper's face.

Fig. 6 is the true symbol: it *is* the thesis made visual.

> "(a--d)~The bank as constructed... (e--h)~The eigen-weight ladder $c_k$ of each"

**Concrete fix:** extract a three-icon motif from Fig. 6 — a window sliding in *time*, a window sliding in *frequency*, three *orthogonal* tapers — and reproduce those three icons at ~1 cm as insets or row labels in Figs. 1, 6, 8, 11, and the two recipe boxes. Three repetitions of a tiny visual will do more for memorability than any additional panel. Currently the reader must hold the three-way correspondence in working memory across fifteen pages.

Secondary point: Fig. 6 currently appears *after* Fig. 9's graphical abstract but the graphical abstract shows curves, not banks. Consider making the graphical abstract a two-row figure: top row the three banks (the mechanism), bottom row the three coincident curves (the consequence). One figure, one symbol.

## Salient — ⚠️
This is the most serious issue. Count the standout ideas on offer:

1. the three methods are one estimator
2. Thomson's eq. (8.3) is unknown to the tutorial literature
3. Slepians are the principal components of a sliding sinc
4. the eigen-weight ladder governs variance
5. Hann+box beats default multitaper on high-dynamic-range spectra
6. the bandwidth rule ($2W \approx$ narrowest feature)
7. a three-route recipe
8. a validated Python package

The paper's own §8 hedges about which matters:

> "the contribution of this paper is the synthesis, the ladder, the bandwidth rule and the recipe rather than any single identity"

That sentence names four contributions as *the* contribution. A reader leaves with a list, not an idea.

**Recommendation:** declare #1 salient and subordinate everything else to it explicitly. Ideas 2–4 are *evidence* for #1; ideas 5–7 are *consequences* of #1; #8 is infrastructure. The intro's five-contribution paragraph currently presents them as peers. Rewrite its first sentence as:

> *"We make one claim and draw four consequences from it. The claim: averaging periodograms, smoothing a periodogram, and multitaper estimation are one estimator — a bank of tapers with weights, built three ways from one window (§5). The consequences: ..."*

Also consider whether the "Slepians are principal components of a sliding sinc" result (§5.3) is strong enough to be its own short companion note. It is the most striking *mathematical* item in the paper and it is currently the fourth thing in a busy section.

## Surprise — ✅
Two genuine hooks, both well-earned and both quantified.

> "the last Slepian taper leaks 6\% of its energy, so with default settings... the Hann-tapered, box-smoothed periodogram beats the multitaper estimate"

> "the Slepian tapers are the principal components of that sliding window"

And the "hidden in plain sight" framing is a third, used with restraint rather than grandstanding:

> "the identity itself occupies half a page in a late section of Thomson's paper and is mentioned in neither tutorial"

I particularly commend the immediate self-limitation:

> "The lesson is not that multitaper estimation is flawed but that the tapered, smoothed periodogram is not a poor cousin. The regime of the claim should be stated exactly..."

That is how to deploy a surprise without losing credibility. Only suggestion: the surprise currently appears in the abstract's *eighth* sentence. Lead paragraph two with it.

## Story — ✅
A clean arc, and unusually the arc is present at both abstract and paper scale. Problem: two parameters with no counterpart in what users already know. Journey: quadratic form → diagonalize → the identity → the sliding window. Resolution: the recipe boxes and the EEG demonstration. The Limitations section is honest and specific ("The EEG evidence is two clips and one channel each") rather than performative.

A genuinely well-constructed unification paper with an honest, generous tone and a real "surprise" payload; its weaknesses are an overloaded abstract, a significance argument that is implied rather than stated, and a prior-work critique that is compressed into one sentence in the intro and deferred to page 10.

## 1. Problem stated clearly and early? — **A−**

**Working well.** The problem lands in the first sentence of the abstract and again at the end of intro ¶1:

> "Yet its two parameters have no counterpart in the first two methods: nothing in ``average the segments'' or ``smooth across frequency'' tells a user what a time--bandwidth product $NW=4$ buys, or what is lost by keeping $K=7$ tapers rather than $6$"

That is a precise, falsifiable statement of a reader's actual confusion. Good.

**Problem.** The abstract's first sentence is ~55 words with two parenthetical attributions embedded, so the problem arrives *stated* but not *felt*.

**Suggested rewrite (abstract opening):**
> "Every user of multitaper spectral estimation must choose a time–bandwidth product $NW$ and a taper count $K$. Neither parameter has any counterpart in the two methods everyone learns first: cutting the record into segments and averaging periodograms (Bartlett, Welch), and smoothing one periodogram across frequency (Daniell, Blackman–Tukey). So a user who already smooths spectrograms cannot tell what multitaper would buy, or cost. We show the three methods are one estimator."

**Also missing:** an explicit early declaration that **no new estimator is proposed**. Readers of a signal-processing paper will scan for a new method and be mildly disoriented. Add one sentence to the contributions paragraph: "We propose no new estimator; we show that three familiar ones are the same one, and price the differences."

---

## 2. Reader told WHY they should care? — **B**

**Working well.** One sentence does almost all the work, and it is the right sentence:

> "The cost of not knowing this is real: a default multitaper estimate on a record of 128 samples has a median error of 12~dB where the tapered, smoothed periodogram has 2~dB."

**Problem.** This arrives at the *end* of intro ¶3, after eight other numbers, and it is the only explicit statement of stakes. The opening — "Spectral analysis is the workhorse of quantitative electrophysiology" — is a throat-clearing generality, not a significance claim. The paper's two real consequences for a reader's life are never stated plainly:

1. *Your existing smoothed spectrogram is already a multitaper estimate; you may say so and quantify its variance.*
2. *Your default `NW=4, K=7` call may be flooring 10 dB above the truth and nothing in the output tells you.*

**Suggested insertion, end of intro ¶1 (before the "three routes" paragraph):**
> "The stakes are practical on both sides. Pipelines that smooth a spectrogram along frequency are routinely told, in tutorials and in review, that they should have used multitaper instead; we show they have computed a multitaper estimate already, and can report its degrees of freedom exactly. Conversely, a record short enough or a spectrum steep enough, and the default multitaper settings floor ten decibels above the truth, with nothing in the output to warn the user. Neither fact is visible from the standard accounts."

**Also:** intro ¶3 packs eleven numbers into five sentences (17.2, 14.0, 8.9, 11.6, 4.6, 1.3, 0.8, 1.5, 0.7, 12, 2). Cut to three headline numbers and push the rest to Table 1; a reader cannot hold this many and will skim past the one that matters.

## 3. Prior work surveyed and critiqued — **B+**

**Working well, and unusually honest.** The critique is exactly the right one and is stated without disparagement:

> "the identity itself occupies half a page in a late section of Thomson's paper and is mentioned in neither tutorial."

And Section 7 is a model of scrupulous crediting, ending:

> "the contribution of this paper is the synthesis, the ladder, the bandwidth rule and the recipe rather than any single identity."

**Problem (Adelson).** The survey-and-critique in the *introduction* is one 80-word sentence that names five sources and critiques none of them individually. The actual survey is Section 7, on roughly page 10 — too late for a reader deciding whether to continue. Adelson wants *why prior work is unsatisfactory* established before the new approach.

**Suggested rewrite (add a short intro paragraph after the "three routes" paragraph):**
> "The pieces of this picture exist but have not been assembled for users. Percival and Walden establish that every quadratic estimator is a weighted multitaper estimator, but do not draw the consequence for the two methods users already run. Riedel and Sidorenko, and Walden, develop the same machinery for kernel and multivariate estimators respectively, in the language of estimation theory rather than of practice. Hansson-Sandsten approximates the multitaper estimator by a Welch structure; we exhibit the window for which the approximation becomes exact. Karnik et al. use the identity as a computational device, not an interpretive one. And the two tutorials that have made multitaper the recommended estimator in neuroscience do not mention it, so the reader who needs it most never meets it. What is missing is the users' account: one bank built three ways, with the variance and leakage of each route priced."

That paragraph costs you 120 words and converts a B+ into an A.

## 4. New approach introduced in the intro? — **A**

> "This paper shows that the three methods are one method (Fig.~\ref{fig:abstract}). All three are quadratic forms in the data, and a quadratic form is a bank of tapers with weights. Averaging periodograms builds the bank by shifting one window along the record; smoothing builds it by shifting one taper along the frequency axis; Thomson builds it from orthogonal tapers."

This is the paper's best passage: the whole mechanism in three clauses, with the parallel structure doing the explaining. Nothing to fix. The five-contribution paragraph is also properly in the intro, not buried.

**Minor:** that contributions paragraph fuses contributions *and* roadmap into one 200-word block. Split into two paragraphs; the roadmap sentence ("Section~\ref{sec:periodogram} sets up...") should stand alone.

## 5. Is it clear why this work is better, and in what specific ways? — **A−**

**Working well.** Three consequences, each quantified, each labeled — and the meta-sentence guiding the reader is excellent craft:

> "Of the three, one is a warning, one is a price, and one is a surprise; if a reader remembers one, it should be the surprise."

The regime of the strongest claim is stated with unusual care:

> "The regime of the claim should be stated exactly: with the default equal weights and $K=2NW-1$, on a spectrum whose dynamic range exceeds the last taper's concentration, the Hann-tapered, box-smoothed periodogram is the better estimate"

**Problem — one overclaim to fix.** The abstract says:

> "We prove that the three methods are one estimator."

But Section 7 says the general fact "is textbook material \cite{percival1993}." The abstract therefore claims as *proved here* something the paper itself credits elsewhere. The intro is honest; the abstract is not, and abstracts are what get quoted back at you.

**Suggested rewrite:** "We show — assembling one identity that Thomson stated in 1982, one general fact that is textbook, and one correspondence that we derive — that the three methods are a single estimator."

**But flag the final sentence:**

### 1. Abstract — subject/verb split across 20 words; the identity itself arrives after the reader's memory is spent
**Original:** "An identity that Thomson stated in 1982, and that the tutorials neuroscientists read do not mention, says that the multitaper estimate with all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, \emph{is} the periodogram averaged over a frequency box of half-width $W$."
**Rewrite:** "Thomson stated the key identity in 1982, and the tutorials neuroscientists read do not mention it: the multitaper estimate with all $N$ Slepian tapers, weighted by their eigenvalues, *is* the periodogram averaged over a frequency box of half-width $W$."
*Colon creates a secondary stress; the identity now lands in the true stress position.*

### 2. §5.4 Welch remark — a 60-word parenthesis separates the claim from its reason
**Original:** "At matched half-power width, Welch's method has the \emph{most} degrees of freedom of any method here ($\nu=17.6$ at a half-power width of $7.8/N$ with segments of $192$ samples in Fig.~8, against $14.0$ at $7.2/N$ for the multitaper estimate at $N=1024$; Table~1 lists both at $N=256$, where the Welch segments are $64$ samples and its half-power width is $5.8/N$; Fig.~S3), because half-overlapping Hann segments are nearly orthogonal."
**Rewrite:** "At matched half-power width, Welch's method has the *most* degrees of freedom of any method here, because half-overlapping Hann segments are nearly orthogonal. In Fig.~8, $\nu=17.6$ at a half-power width of $7.8/N$ (segments of 192 samples), against $14.0$ at $7.2/N$ for the multitaper estimate; Table~1 and Fig.~S3 give the same comparison at $N=256$, where the segments are 64 samples wide and the half-power width is $5.8/N$."

### 3. §5.4 — longest subject–verb separation in the paper
**Original:** "a least-squares search over segment windows and over taper--kernel pairs for the quadratic form closest to that of \eqref{eq:mt} returns, in both families, essentially the sliding sinc and the rectangular taper with the box (relative Frobenius distance about $0.27$ to $0.29$, against $0.86$ for the Hann taper with the box; ...)."
**Rewrite:** "we searched both families — segment windows, and taper–kernel pairs — for the quadratic form closest to that of \eqref{eq:mt}, and in both the winner is essentially the leaky estimator itself: the sliding sinc, and the rectangular taper with the box (relative Frobenius distance 0.27–0.29, against 0.86 for the Hann taper with the box; ...)."
*The finding — that the nearest reachable form is the leaky one — now occupies the stress position instead of a parenthesis of distances.*

**B4. Riedel–Sidorenko is cited twice for different things but never compared numerically.** Sine tapers appear only in supplementary Fig. S3. Since Riedel–Sidorenko's minimum-bias tapers and their "a few tapers then smooth" recommendation are the direct competitors to your everyday trio, give sine tapers one row in Table 1 and one clause in §5.4. Riedel–Sidorenko–Thomson's finding ("best of all") is currently reported and then dropped — it deserves a quantified reply: what is $\nu$ and leakage for "$K=3$ Slepians then a box" at $2W=8/N$? If that hybrid dominates everything in your table, saying so is a stronger paper, not a weaker one.

**B5. Equation (10) needs an attribution.** $\nu = 2(\sum c_k)^2/\sum c_k^2$ is the Welch–Satterthwaite effective-degrees-of-freedom approximation, and in this setting it is standard (Percival & Walden give the $\nu = (\mathrm{tr}\,Q)^2/\mathrm{tr}\,Q^2$ form). Naming the quantity "the eigen-weight ladder" and "identifying" it as a contribution without citing Satterthwaite/Welch is the single most likely trigger for a "this is known" review. Cite it, then claim the *use* — that the ladder's slope, not the taper family, explains the single-taper variance deficit — which is a legitimate and pedagogically valuable framing.

## C. Missing related work (grouped; each is likely to be raised)

**C1. The neuroscience lineage is under-cited.** The paper's target audience is set by two tutorials, but the canonical multitaper-in-neuroscience papers and the actual pipelines are absent:
- Mitra & Pesaran (1999), *Analysis of dynamic brain imaging data*, Biophys J — the paper that put multitaper into systems neuroscience; Bokil et al. (2010) Chronux.
- Oostenveld et al. (2011) FieldTrip (`mtmconvol`), Gramfort et al. (2013) MNE-Python, Prerau's multitaper toolbox, `scipy.signal.welch`. Your claim that "many pipelines do this to a spectrogram without naming it" is an empirical claim about software and should be supported by naming the software and its defaults (which $NW$, which $K$, which smoother). This would sharpen the "cost of not knowing" argument considerably.

**C2. A direct precedent in genre that you should embrace, not fear.** Bruns (2004), *Fourier-, Hilbert- and wavelet-based signal analysis: are they really different approaches?* (J Neurosci Methods) is the same move — "these three neuroscience methods are one" — in the same audience. Citing it positions your paper in a recognizable and well-received tradition. Relatedly, a sentence on where wavelet/Morlet and Hilbert-envelope spectrograms sit relative to your bank picture (a Morlet transform is a frequency-dependent-bandwidth sliding window, i.e. your route (b′) with $W \propto f$) would substantially widen the paper's reach and costs one paragraph.

**C3. Prewhitening is the missing classical remedy, and its absence makes one headline look like a straw man.** "The box-smoothed untapered periodogram floors 20 dB high" is true, but the classical answer to high dynamic range is not only tapering — it is prewhitening (Percival & Walden ch. 6; Blackman–Tukey's own advice). A reviewer with a geophysics or statistics background will say no competent user smooths an untapered periodogram of a 65-dB spectrum. Either (i) add a sentence in §7 or Limitations acknowledging prewhitening as the third classical route to leakage control and noting it is orthogonal to your trio, or better (ii) add one curve: AR(4) prewhitened by an AR(1) filter, untapered, box-smoothed. If it tracks the truth, your "without a taper, wrong" claim needs the qualifier "and without prewhitening"; that honesty will strengthen the rest.

**C4. Bandwidth choice has a literature you are not engaging.** You present the "set $2W$ to the narrowest feature that matters" rule as a contribution while the statistics literature has decades of automatic span/bandwidth selection: Wahba (1980), Hurvich (1985), Lee (1997) span selection for periodogram smoothing, Ombao et al. data-driven bandwidth, and on the multitaper side Haley & Anitescu (2017) optimal bandwidth, Barbour & Parker (2014) adaptive sine multitaper, Prieto et al. (2009) quadratic multitaper with derivative corrections. You need not adopt any of them, but §4.4 and §7 must acknowledge them and say why a judgement rule is preferable for your audience (interpretability; the optimum is broad; the "narrowest feature that matters" is a scientific rather than statistical choice). As written, "the bandwidth rule" is the most vulnerable of your five claimed contributions.

**C5. Window-design facts want the standard reference.** Hann $-31$ dB first side lobe, $1/f^6$ tails, Bohman comparisons, half-power widths, overlap correlation — cite Harris (1978) and/or Nuttall (1981) rather than leaving these as unsourced folklore.

**C6. Adaptive weighting is central to your main claim and cited only in passing.** Since the "Hann+box beats multitaper" result is explicitly a claim about defaults, Thomson & Chave (1991) (adaptive weights, jackknife confidence intervals) and Thomson (1990) quadratic-inverse should be cited where you say adaptive weights "repair" the leakage — and see D2 below.

**C7. Multivariate/coherence.** Walden (2000) is cited for the algebra but the practical consequence is unmentioned: for coherence and cross-spectra, multitaper's orthogonal bank has advantages that a single-taper smoother does not share (and coherence bias depends on $\nu$, which your ladder computes). One sentence in §7 plus one in Limitations; otherwise a connectivity-oriented reviewer will call the recipe incomplete.

## D. Limitations: honest and well-scoped, with four real gaps

The section is a model of specificity in tone (adaptive weights not implemented; "a claim about defaults"; two clips, one channel; stationarity violated at seizure onset). Keep all of it. Missing:

**D1. Metric and stimulus dependence of the headline comparison.** The 11.6 / 4.6 / 1.3 / 0.8 dB numbers come from *one* AR(4) spectrum with 65 dB range, scored as median absolute dB error over the whole band — a metric dominated by the low-power region where leakage lives, i.e. the metric most favourable to your conclusion. Say so, and ideally report the same ranking under one alternative (RMS in dB over the peak region only, or over 300 realizations as in Fig. 2) so a reviewer cannot claim the comparison was engineered. Also note that Fig. 8's numbers come from a single realization while Fig. 3's come from 300 — inconsistent, and a reviewer will notice.

**D2. The comparison is not at matched $\nu$.** Hann+box has $\nu=8.9$ and multitaper $K=7$ has $\nu=14.0$; you compare them at matched *bandwidth*. That is a defensible choice, but the limitation should state that at matched $\nu$ (i.e., widening the box until $\nu=14$) the bias comparison changes, and say in which direction.

**D3. Unsupported speed claim.** §6 recommends route (c′) "for speed" with no timing, in a paper that cites a fast multitaper algorithm (Karnik et al.). Either add a one-line benchmark (FFT+convolution vs $K$ FFTs vs Karnik) or drop the word.

**D4. No inference.** The paper stops at $\nu$ and never discusses confidence intervals, the $\chi^2_\nu$-based error bars that $\nu$ licenses for *all three* routes, Thomson's F-test for line components (mentioned once in the recipe), or jackknifing. For EEG users testing ictal band-power changes, "your smoother's $\nu$ tells you your CI" is a practical payoff that costs two sentences and pre-empts "so what do I report?"

Minor: the limitation "Frequency-dependent bandwidths ... is a reason to prefer that route rather than a limitation of the analysis" is a small piece of salesmanship inside the limitations section. Move the sales part to the Discussion and keep only "not studied here."

## E. Conclusion: strong voice, narrow horizon

It does not end with a whimper — "with a taper, as good on leakage and a third short on degrees of freedom; without one, wrong" is the right kind of sentence, and the closing clause about choosing on grounds of convenience lands.

Two improvements:
1. **Widen the last beat.** The conclusion's scope is "For most EEG work any of the three is fine." The paper supports something larger: that spectral-estimator choice is a *convenience* decision in a broad class of analyses, which means cross-pipeline reproducibility of band-power results, and that the bank-and-ladder picture prices any smoother a pipeline applies — including ones nobody has named. End there rather than on EEG.
2. **Do not re-open the "beats multitaper" claim without its qualifier.** The conclusion's "without one, wrong" is fine, but the abstract's "beats the multitaper estimate" should carry, in the abstract itself, the two qualifiers that §5.4 and Limitations correctly supply: default equal weights with $K=2NW-1$, **and** at lower $\nu$. As written the abstract oversells by one clause, and that clause is what a skeptical reviewer will quote.
